The highest-intent searches in most B2B categories are comparisons. "Salesforce alternative for small teams." "Figma vs Penpot for design systems." "Notion competitors that work offline." The person typing that has a credit card and a grievance — the two conditions of a purchase — and most companies cede those queries to affiliates and review farms because writing about competitors feels dirty or dangerous.

It is neither, done right. We've written about [building individual alternative pages that rank without embarrassment](/journal/growth/competitor-alternatives-pages); this is the next step: running comparisons as a *programme* — dozens of pages, maintained, measured, governed by editorial rules strict enough that competitors' fans read them and nod. The programme is where the compounding happens, and where most attempts fall apart.

## The research: intent before volume

Comparison keywords live in five shapes, and the programme starts by cataloguing which ones exist in your category:

1. **"[Competitor] alternative"** — active dissatisfaction or procurement diligence. Highest intent, usually highest volume.
2. **"[Competitor A] vs [Competitor B]"** — mid-funnel evaluating. You get included editorially or you get ignored.
3. **"How to switch from [Competitor]"** — decision made, logistics feared. Migration guides; criminally underserved.
4. **"Best [category] for [segment]"** — list intent, owned by review farms. Winnable only with genuine segment focus.
5. **"[Competitor] pricing"** — people sense opacity. Addressing it (fairly) is a trust goldmine.

Research the set with the usual tools, but weight by intent, not volume: fifty searches a month for "[giant rival] alternative for [your niche]" outranks five thousand for "[giant rival] login." Then **verify by listening**: your sales calls already contain the real comparisons — which rivals get named, in what tone, against which objections. Mining those calls is its own discipline (the playbook on [customer stories that rank](/journal/growth/customer-stories-that-rank) shows the mining-and-recording method), and it tells you which comparisons people actually make versus which ones the keyword tools invent.

Prioritise into the first two dozen pages: the competitors you genuinely replace in deals, the segments where you genuinely win, the migrations you can genuinely document.

## The editorial rules (the part competitors' fans check)

The programme's moat is a ruleset every page obeys, because comparison content is audited by the most hostile readership in marketing: people who love the competitor. Rules we've settled:

- **Lead with who the competitor is genuinely right for.** Not as throat-clearing — really. "If you need 10,000-person webinar support, ToolCo is the correct choice." A comparison page that concedes real strengths buys the credibility to state real weaknesses. The pattern we wrote about in [comparison pages that convert without lying](/journal/web-design/comparison-pages-that-convert) applies at programme scale: honesty is the conversion strategy, not a style choice.
- **Cite versions and dates.** "As of March 2026, on their Growth plan…" Comparison claims rot; a page that knows its own timestamp is trusted and a page without one is assumed stale — because it usually is.
- **Quote their actual pricing page, linked.** Never paraphrase competitor pricing from memory or a two-year-old blog post; it's the single fastest way to be wrong in public.
- **No invented reviews.** If you include user quotes, they're sourced from public reviews with attribution and dates. Fake balance — inventing negatives about your own product to seem fair — is its own category of lying, and readers smell it.
- **The decision framework matters more than the verdict.** Pages that say "choose X if, choose us if, choose Y if" convert better than pages that conclude "we're best." Decision content respects the reader; verdict content flatters the writer. We design these as [comparison tables that carry the decision](/journal/web-design/comparison-tables-marketing) — the table is where the fairness is *auditable* at a glance.

One more rule, the one legal teams always ask about and marketing teams always forget: **their trademarks in your metadata and copy are fine for nominative comparison; their logos, screenshots beyond fair-dealing necessity, and any implication of affiliation are not.** Your lawyer confirms the local version of that sentence once, and the rules sheet carries it thereafter.

## The operating system: freshness as a job

Here's why most comparison programmes die: pages are published, competitors change pricing, change features, get acquired — and six months later your "fair" page is an unfair relic ranking #1 for queries it now deserves to lose. Stale comparison content is worse than none: it erodes trust at the exact highest-intent moment.

So the programme includes maintenance as a budgeted line, not an aspiration:

- **Quarterly re-verification sweep.** Every page, manually re-checked against the competitor's current site and pricing. Automated diff-monitoring of rival pricing pages helps (a scraper watching for changes), but a human re-reads the page.
- **A changelog per page.** "Updated April 2026: Acme raised Team plan pricing; added their new offline mode." Visible update logs build reader trust and give you a recrawl signal — the same compounding mechanism as [refreshing old content](/journal/growth/refreshing-old-content-wins), amplified by the churn rate of your competitors' marketing.
- **Deprecation protocol.** When a competitor dies, pivots, or becomes irrelevant, redirect or rewrite the page rather than leaving it ranking on a corpse. Cadence-wise, plan on each page needing an hour per quarter and a rebuild every 18 months; budget-approved programme, not a content hobby.

Careful with scale temptation here. When someone suggests generating 500 "[X] alternative" pages programmatically, remember the [programmatic SEO ethics](/journal/growth/programmatic-seo-ethics) line: a template that multiplies pages without multiplying research produces landfill with your logo on it. The programme scales by quarter and by verified rival, not by spreadsheet explode.

## Measuring the programme honestly

Comparison pages need their own measurement frame, because their job is unusual: they intercept people already considering someone else. We track:

- **Assisted conversions, not just last-click.** These pages sit mid-journey; attribution that credits only the final touch will undervalue them permanently, which is why our stance on [attribution that admits uncertainty](/journal/growth/attribution-models-honest) matters here.
- **Win language in sales calls.** Reps report when prospects arrive pre-argued with your comparison page's framing. That's the signal money can't dashboard.
- **Brand-search remix.** Watch "[your brand] vs [rival]" queries grow — comparison content often *creates* the comparison it then wins.
- **The trust metric that isn't a metric: inbound corrections.** When a competitor's employee emails you "your page says X, it's now Y" — and they say it politely — your programme has achieved reference status. Fix it the same day and log it in the page changelog; the fastest correction wins respect from the only audience segment that will never forgive a mistake.

A comparison programme done this way is slow to build and very hard to displace: every quarter of honest maintenance is a moat of trust the review farms can't copy and the affiliate sites can't fake. In a category where buyers arrive with a grievance and a shortlist, being the fairest voice in the comparison is the most durable growth position there is.

## Key takeaways

- Comparison queries are the highest-intent searches in most B2B categories; research by intent shape (alternative / vs / switch / best-for / pricing), weighted by real sales-call evidence.
- Rank the first two dozen pages by who you genuinely replace, not by keyword volume.
- Editorial rules are the moat: concede strengths, cite versions and dates, link their pricing, never invent reviews, decide frameworks over verdicts.
- Fairness is a freshness problem: quarterly verification sweeps, visible per-page changelogs, deprecation protocol. Budget the hours or don't start.
- Attribution will under-credit these pages — measure assisted conversions, sales-call win language, and the politeness of competitors' corrections.

## FAQ

**Won't these pages just send traffic to competitors?**
Occasionally, a reader concludes the rival fits them better — which is a sales call you didn't waste and a churn you didn't earn. The arithmetic has always favoured the honest filter: comparison pages attract *in-market* readers you'd rarely otherwise touch, and being the trusted frame for the decision beats being absent from it.

**Do we need our competitors' permission or legal review per page?**
One legal review of the *rules* (nominative use, no logo misuse, dated claims, no affiliation implication), then the rules govern pages — escalating only when a claim shape is new. Per-page lawyering is how programmes die quietly; rules-based governance is how they survive procurement-scale caution.

**Should we run paid ads on competitor terms too?**
Different play with shared research: the bidding decision has its own economics (we cover the adjacent question in [bidding on your own name](/journal/growth/brand-term-bidding-decision)), but bidding on rival terms while your organic comparison page ranks #1 often buys little. Fund both from the same keyword research and let the organic fairness carry the trust the ad can't.

**We're the category leader being targeted by challengers' comparison pages. Respond?**
Publicly, almost never — rebuttals platform the challenger. Privately, treat inaccurate rival pages as free research: their claims are your product's perceived weaknesses, focus-grouped at their expense. Fix the product story where they're right, tighten your own "switching from us" content where saves matter, and let their readers discover your pricing transparency page on their own.
