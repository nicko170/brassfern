Every marketing site is written twice. First by the company, in its internal dialect — "streamline," "empower," "end-to-end visibility" — and then, if someone does the work, in the dialect customers actually speak. The gap between those two dialects is where conversion rates go to die.

The good news: your customers have already written your best copy, out loud, on recorded calls with sales, in onboarding surveys, in support tickets, in the reason field of your churn form. Voice-of-customer (VoC) mining is the discipline of extracting that language and turning it into pages. It's not a new invention — copywriters have done it with notebooks since before the web — but recordings and transcription have made it systematic, and systematic is where the edge lives.

## Sources and consent, first

Before any methodology: the plumbing and the ethics.

**Record with real consent, in the spirit, not just the letter.** "This call may be recorded" said in a robot voice at second two is technically consent and practically a cold open that changes the call. Better: the rep says it like a human — "mind if we record this? It helps us not take bad notes" — and the recording policy lives somewhere a customer could actually find it, consistent with whatever your [privacy commitments](/legal/privacy) promise. AI notetakers that join unannounced are a brand decision, not a tooling decision; plenty of prospects now see the bot join and grade you on it.

**The sources, ranked by density of copy-worthy language:**

1. **Sales and discovery calls** — the richest vein. People explain problems the way they'd explain them to a friend when money is at stake.
2. **Onboarding kickoffs** — why they *bought*, in past-tense hindsight. Different and valuable from why they'd buy.
3. **Exit/churn conversations and win-loss interviews** — the unvarnished stuff, always in sharper language than surveys. (Building these as a practice is its own discipline; see [exit interviews for products](/journal/growth/churn-interviews-exit-surveys).)
4. **Support tickets and chat logs** — problem language at its most urgent and concrete.
5. **Review sites and community threads** — your customers *and your competitors' customers*, which is market research nobody commissioned.

Survey verbatims sit last on purpose: written answers compress toward politeness. Spoken language is where the blood is.

## The mining pass: tagging what matters

The raw material is useless as transcripts-in-a-folder. The mining pass turns it into an asset. For each source, you extract snippets — verbatim sentences, with speaker context — into a tagged store (a table in your research tool; a spreadsheet honestly works). Three tag axes cover nearly everything copy needs:

- **Pain vs goal vs objection.** Pains describe the before ("I was rebuilding the report in Excel every Monday, three hours"); goals describe the after ("I just want it to be *done* when I open my laptop"); objections describe the friction ("last time we switched, the migration ate a quarter"). Headlines draw mostly from goals and pains; [landing-page sections](/journal/web-design/landing-page-anatomy) address objections in the FAQ and the proof areas.
- **Topic.** Which feature, which workflow, which competitor. This is what lets a tag store answer "what do people actually say about onboarding?" across forty calls.
- **Heat.** Did they lean in? Profanity, laughter, repetition, the phrase said twice for emphasis ("it was *so* manual") — mark intensity, because intensity is sorting weight for copy later.

The rule that keeps the store honest: **verbatim or nothing.** The moment taggers paraphrase, the dialect dies and you're back to internal language with extra steps. Capture "we were drowning in CSV exports," not "experienced reporting difficulties."

Volume guidance: twenty to thirty mined sources is where patterns emerge; the marginal value stays high through about sixty, then flattens. This is a quarterly practice, not a one-off research project — dialect drifts with the market.

## From verbatims to headlines: the translation rules

The temptation is copy-paste: find a great quote, make it the hero headline. Resist it half the time. Raw verbatims carry the truth but not always the shape; a headline is a compression with grammar. The workflow:

1. **Pattern-find first.** Sort the pains by frequency × heat. The headline should come from the *modal* customer language — what five people said in similar words — not the single most poetic quote. The poet is the outlier; the chorus is the market. (The segue to [conversion copywriting principles](/journal/growth/conversion-copywriting) is short: clarity over cleverness, always, but clarity *in their words*.)
2. **Compress toward their nouns and verbs, not yours.** Customer said "I rebuild the same report every Monday" → the headline candidate is "Stop rebuilding the same report every Monday", not "Automate recurring reporting workflows." Keep the day of the week in it. Specificity is the smell of truth to a scanning reader; "Monday" outconverts "repeatedly" every time we've tested it.
3. **Match claim strength to evidence.** A verbatim can prove a feeling; it cannot prove your product delivers it. The goal-language becomes your headline's *promise shape*, then the page earns it with proof — demo, numbers, the [case study](/work). VoC tells you what people ache for; it doesn't license you to claim the ache is solved. That distinction is the entire line between copy that converts and copy that gets sceptics screenshotting you.

## Feeding the mine back into the machine

Mined language shouldn't stop at static pages — it's the highest-quality input several programmes have:

- **The message test queue.** Every compressed headline candidate goes into the experimentation backlog as a hypothesis *with its evidence attached*: "5 of 22 discovery calls, heat: high." VoC-sourced variants win page tests at a meaningfully higher rate than brainstormed ones, because the hypothesis started closer to the money. Design the test honourably — pre-registered, sized properly, the discipline in [A/B testing statistics for people who ship](/journal/growth/ab-testing-honest-statistics).
- **Sales enablement, mirrored back.** The same digest that feeds the site feeds the sales deck; prospects hearing on calls the phrases they used on the last call is the best demo opener there is. Mined language is also how you write [comparison and alternative pages](/journal/growth/competitor-alternatives-pages) that sound like a peer instead of an ambush.
- **The message-market check.** When the patterns shift — a new pain outranks the old across a quarter's mining — that's an early signal your positioning is aging, arriving months before the conversion rate reports it. The mine is a sensor network, not just a copy machine.

## The operating rhythm

Folding this into an actual week: one person owns the mine (in house or an agency partner), the pass is two hours per source at most, the store gets a groom monthly — merge duplicate tags, retire stale dialect, re-rank heat — and a quarterly "language review" lands the top patterns in front of whoever owns the homepage. Between quarters, reps keep recording; the corpus compounds quietly. VoC mining is unglamorous — no tool makes the listening skippable, no AI summary replaces hearing five strangers say "Monday" with feeling. But it's the closest thing marketing has to sitting inside the customer's head with a notebook, and the pages it produces read like they remembered being talked *at* is the thing everyone hates. Because they were written in the reader's own voice, they read like being understood.

## Key takeaways

- Your best converting copy already exists: spoken, unprompted, in sales calls, onboardings, exits and tickets. Writing the site twice — internal dialect first, customer dialect second — is the job.
- Record with human-sounding consent; rank sources by language density (calls > exits > tickets > reviews > surveys).
- Tag verbatims on three axes — pain/goal/objection, topic, heat — and never paraphrase in the store.
- Headlines come from the modal language, compressed; keep their nouns, their verbs and their "every Monday."
- Verbatims prove the ache, not your solution — headlines promise, pages prove.
- Feed the mine into tests, decks and positioning reviews; a quarterly language shift in the corpus is your earliest warning system.

## FAQ

**Can AI transcribe-and-summarise tools just do the mining?**
Transcription, yes — it's table stakes now, and searchable text is what makes sixty calls mineable. Summarisation, carefully: summaries paraphrase by design, and dialect dies in paraphrase. Use AI to *retrieve* (every moment anyone said "audit"," "spreadsheet," "Monday") and to cluster, then read the raw verbatims yourself before any of it touches a headline. The tool accelerates the search; judgment remains the job.

**We have no sales calls recorded yet. Can we start from surveys?**
You can start, but treat survey verbatims as scaffolding; their compression and politeness bias toward slogans. Faster starts: mine review sites (yours and competitors' — the language is real and public), support tickets, and run five open-question customer interviews this month rather than a survey to five hundred. Five conversations beat five hundred checkboxes for this purpose every time.

**Isn't using customer quotes as headlines a kind of appropriation?**
Not if you keep the boundary between *language* (shared, transformed into your page copy) and *attribution* (using someone's name, face or verbatim words publicly, which needs permission). VoC mining uses the former — the phrases become yours the way accents do. The moment a real quote appears on the site with attribution implied, get written consent or cut it.

**How does this apply before we have customers at all?**
Mine the *adjacent* corpus: the subreddits, review sections and support forums of whatever duct-tape solution your future customers use today. Pre-launch VoC is entirely doable — people describe pains about existing tools with no restraint whatsoever — and it's the same reason competitor review-mining powers [comparison-page programmes](/journal/growth/alternative-to-pages-program) from day one.
