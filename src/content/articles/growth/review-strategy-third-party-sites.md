There's a moment in every B2B buying process that vendors can't see and can't fully control: the shortlisting stakeholder opens a new tab and types your name plus a review platform into a search box. What loads in that tab might be an honest cross-section of your customer base, or it might be the four angriest people who ever used your product plus one suspicious five-star from 2021 that reads like a press release. Which tab the buyer finds is not luck. It's the output of a system you either built or neglected.

Third-party review presence is a growth channel wearing reputation's clothes. Buyers trust it because they don't believe you. The goal is not a perfect rating — nobody believes a perfect rating — it's a representative one: recent, specific, spread across the platforms buyers in your category actually check, and honest enough that the four-stars do the convincing.

## Map the battlefield before you recruit

Different categories live on different platforms, and the mistake is treating "reviews" as one thing. Software buyers triangulate across the big B2B review sites plus, increasingly, community threads. Local and hospitality live in the dominant map app's reviews. E-commerce has marketplace reviews and post-purchase platforms. Your first move is boring and decisive: search the way your buyers search — "[category] software", "[your name] reviews", "[competitor] vs" — and inventory what surfaces on page one. Those platforms, in that order, are the battlefield.

Two corollaries. Concentrate: fifty reviews on the one platform that ranks beats fifteen each on four — review volume is per-platform social proof, and dilution looks like absence. And watch [where comparison pages rank](/journal/growth/competitor-alternatives-pages), because review platforms own a large fraction of "X vs Y" and "alternatives to X" real estate; your presence there is alternately defence and offence.

## Timing the ask to delivered value

The default review ask — a quarterly email blast to the whole customer list — optimises for the sender's calendar and lands at emotionally random moments. Some recipients just had a great week with your product; some have an open support ticket. The blast is how you collect reviews that say "fine, I guess."

The better mechanism is trigger-based: ask at the moment of demonstrated value, when the customer just did something they'll happily talk about. Inventory your product and service for these moments — they're specific to each business, but the pattern is universal:

- **The milestone event.** First payroll run, first month of closed books, tenth campaign shipped, first NPS promoter score given. Whatever your product's "we did the thing" moment is, it is the ask window.
- **The unsolicited compliment.** A customer says something kind in support, on a call, in a thread. The reply is: "That means a lot — would you mind saying it on [platform]? Here's a direct link." No lag, no blast, strike while warm.
- **The renewal or expansion.** A signature is a literal vote. Asking two weeks after renewal — "now that we're into year two" — converts at rates that make blast scheduling look like a prank.

Cadence matters more than volume. A steady drip of two-to-four reviews a month reads as a living product; a campaign spike of forty followed by silence reads as a campaign — to buyers *and* to the platforms, whose fraud filters have seen every spike pattern ever mailed.

## Make it easy, never scripted

Friction is the enemy; scripting is the enemy wearing a helpful hat. The line between them:

Easy means: a direct link to the review form for your listing (not the platform homepage, not a scavenger hunt), a stated time expectation ("takes three minutes"), asked through the relationship that exists (CSM email, in-app prompt after a milestone, not a stranger), exactly once, with one gentle reminder. You're removing administrative friction from a favour.

Scripted means: suggested text, star-rating steering ("if you had a great experience, leave us a review — if not, contact us first"), incentives contingent on positivity, or a marketing-crafted template the customer is invited to "adapt." Beyond violating most platforms' terms, scripted language produces that telltale sound — a paragraph that reads like brochure — which savvy buyers distrust more than they distrust a missing review. The gating question is the same one we'd ask of any conversion copy: does it sound like a person who uses the product, or like the product? The discipline of keeping customer voice intact is worth stealing from [voice-of-customer work](/journal/growth/voice-of-customer-mining): verbatim or nothing.

And the bright line: **never pay for positive reviews, never gate negative ones.** Gating solicitation by sentiment — asking promoters to review and routing detractors to a private form — is review fraud in a business-casual outfit, buyers can smell the resulting too-clean profile, and several platforms will delist you for it. Ask everyone the same way, at the moment of value, and let the distribution be honest.

## Negative reviews are the audition

Here's the thing nobody reviews-strategising wants to hear: a representative profile includes one-stars, and that's the good news. Buyers don't read five-star reviews to decide; they read the *negative* ones — to learn the failure modes, and to watch how the company behaves under criticism. Your response to a bad review is not customer service. It's a public audition for every future buyer who reads it.

The response discipline:

1. **Respond to every negative review, fast-ish.** Days, not weeks. A three-month-later reply signals that nobody's home.
2. **A named human, not a brand.** "Hi, I'm Priya, I run growth at Brassfern" beats "We at Brassfern value your feedback" by the distance between a handshake and a hold message.
3. **Acknowledge the specific complaint without defensiveness.** The reader is testing whether you're safe to have a problem with. "You're right, our onboarding documentation for that flow was genuinely thin" is the sentence that wins the room.
4. **State what changed, only if it changed.** A fix you can point to is gold. If it's unresolved, say what's happening now. Never promise; always report.
5. **Offer the continuation privately, persist the record publicly.** Take resolution off-platform, but leave the exchange standing. Buying committees screenshot responses.

And when the complaint is true at the product level — when three reviews say the same thing — that's not a reputation problem, it's a roadmap input. Route recurring review themes into the same analysis as [exit interviews and churn conversations](/journal/growth/churn-interviews-exit-surveys); a review platform is a churn interview the customer gave to the public instead of to you.

## Close the loop: reviews back into positioning

The last step is the one that turns reputation management into growth: review language is positioning research you didn't commission. Quarterly, mine the corpus — yours and your closest competitors'. What do the five-stars praise in their own words? That's your differentiation, stated by the market; check whether your homepage says it. What do the middling reviews wish for? That's your comparison-page content and your objection handling. What do competitors' one-stars complain about? That's a message you may have earned the right to make — carefully, factually, no glee.

Platforms permitting, the strongest quotes flow back onto your site as attributed proof — a real name and company beside a real sentence outperforms any testimonial paragraph the brand writes about itself, and it's the raw material for [customer stories that actually rank and convert](/journal/growth/customer-stories-that-rank). The loop, closed: delivered value earns reviews, reviews teach you what the delivered value actually was, positioning sharpens, the next reviews get more specific. A review programme done right is not a chore you do for the algorithm; it's the market narrating your [work](/work) back to you, in public, for free.

## Key takeaways

- Audit first: search like your buyers, inventory which platforms own page one in your category, and concentrate volume there — dilution looks like absence.
- Ask at moments of demonstrated value (milestones, compliments, renewals) through existing relationships; steady drips beat campaign spikes.
- Easy, never scripted: direct links and honest asks are fine; suggested text, steering and sentiment-gated solicitation are fraud-shaped and buyers can tell.
- Negative reviews are an audition for future buyers: respond fast, named-human, specific, with change you can point to.
- Mine the review corpus quarterly — it's free positioning research, comparison-page ammunition and testimonial gold.

## FAQ

**Can we offer incentives for reviews?**
For honest reviews, occasionally yes — where the platform's terms allow it, disclosed, and explicitly incentive-for-a-review-not-a-rating ("review us honestly and here's a small thank-you"). Never contingent on positivity, never on major platforms that prohibit incentives outright. When in doubt, don't: the asymmetry is brutal. A delisting or a "this company buys reviews" thread costs more than any incentive converted.

**A competitor's profile is clearly astroturfed. Do we respond in kind?**
No — you respond with patience and, where it's egregious, a platform report. Fake-velocity profiles decay: buyers learn to smell uniform five-star prose, platforms purge in waves, and the company has bought itself a cliff. Your forty real reviews with a lived-in 4.6 will beat their two hundred suspicious 5.0s in every buying committee that does its homework. The honest profile is the durable moat here, which is a rare treat.

**How many reviews before we're 'credible'?**
Category-relative, not absolute. Outrun the median of your realistic shortlist set, not the market leader with a ten-year head start. In most niches, thirty-to-fifty recent, specific reviews on the primary platform clears the credibility bar for a buying committee, and velocity from there matters more than the count — five this quarter reads healthier than fifty in 2023 and silence since.

**Should we respond to positive reviews too?**
Briefly, and not all of them — a human thank-you on the substantial ones, silence on drive-bys. Save your words for the negative and the detailed. A company that replies to everything reads as automation; a company that replies thoughtfully where it matters reads as staffed by adults, which is, not coincidentally, what buyers are trying to determine.
