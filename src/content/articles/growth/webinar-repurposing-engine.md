A good webinar is the most content-dense hour a marketing team ever produces — and the most wasted. The standard lifecycle is tragic: four weeks of promotion, one hour of live delivery, a recording posted to a page nobody visits, and then the whole thing sinks beneath the next campaign. You've compressed forty hours of subject-matter expertise, audience questions and live demonstration into an asset with the surface area of a thumbnail.

The fix is a **repurposing engine**: a standing system that slices every event into a portfolio of assets, each mapped to a channel and a search intent, produced in days rather than months. This isn't "post the clip on LinkedIn". It's an industrial process for respect — for the expert's time, the audience's questions and your own publishing calendar. Here's the engine we run, and the boring operational details that make it actually run.

## Start at the brief, not the recording

Repurposing fails retrospectively. If you plan the slices after the event, you get whatever the conversation happened to contain. So the engine starts when the webinar is commissioned: every event brief includes a **target asset list** — the three articles, the four clips, the FAQ post this event is expected to yield. This changes how the event itself is run. The host asks the questions the FAQ post needs. The guest is briefed to deliver self-contained answers ("say that again as a complete sentence" is a legitimate production note). One segment is explicitly staged as the demo clip.

This is the same principle as our [content strategy planning](/journal/growth/content-strategy-compounds): distribution is designed upstream of production, never bolted on after. A webinar briefed for repurposing yields twice the usable material of one that isn't, from the same hour.

## The slicing taxonomy

One sixty-minute event, twelve asset types — we rarely produce all twelve, but the menu is fixed so the choice is fast:

**Search assets (compounding, slow burn)**
1. **The flagship article.** The event's core argument, rewritten as a proper piece — not a transcript with headings. 1,200–1,800 words, targeting the primary search intent. Transcripts are raw material, not content; nobody has ever subscribed to a transcript.
2. **The FAQ post.** Every audience question, answered properly, internally linked. These pages are unglamorous and rank for years, because they're literally the market's questions in the market's words.
3. **The dissent piece.** The sharpest moment of disagreement, expanded into a standalone opinion article. Controversy that happened live reads as conviction in print.

**Distribution assets (fast, feed-native)**
4. **Three to five clips, 45–90 seconds**, each one idea with burned-in captions, cut for silence-on autoplay. One clip, one idea — a clip with three ideas has zero.
5. **Quote graphics** for the two most repeatable lines. Unglamorous, reliable, cheap.
6. **The newsletter lead.** The event's best insight as the next [newsletter](/journal/growth/newsletter-growth-engine) issue's opening essay, linking to the flagship article, not the recording.

**Sales and enablement assets (quietly valuable)**
7. **The objections one-pager** for sales: every hard question asked live, with the strong answer, formatted for a deal thread.
8. **The onboarding clip** — if the event demoed a workflow, the cleanest two minutes becomes product-adjacent help content.
9. **The internal enablement cut** for new hires: the event is usually the company's clearest statement on its topic; don't keep it a secret from your own team.

**Long-tail assets (optional, opportunistic)**
10–12. LinkedIn carousel from the framework slides, a community-forum answer pack, and — if the topic earned it — the seed of a downloadable guide built from two or three events on a theme.

The point of a fixed menu: decision fatigue is what kills repurposing programmes. "What can we make from this?" is a meeting. "Which five of the twelve does this event yield?" is a checkbox.

## Map every asset to an intent before you publish

Each asset gets one line in the tracking sheet: target keyword or channel, and what a reader should do next. The flagship article targets the head term and links to the relevant [service](/services/growth) or product page. The FAQ post targets the question variants and links up to the flagship. The clips link to the article, never to the raw recording page. This is the same [cluster logic](/journal/growth/content-clusters-strategy) you'd apply to any content family — repurposed assets just arrive as a ready-made cluster, which is their hidden superpower: a webinar produces its own internal-linking neighbourhood in one stroke.

One rule about the recording itself: keep the page, but don't expect it to rank or convert. Give the recording page a strong written summary, embed the clips, and treat it as the archive hub the other assets reference. The recording is the source of the mine, not the product.

## Tooling that isn't a full-time job

The operational trap is turning this into a production department. Our engine runs on deliberately boring tools:

- **Transcription** — any decent speech-to-text service; the transcript feeds the writer who drafts the flagship article and the FAQ post. A human writes; the machine types.
- **Clipping** — one editor, one templated caption style, a hard rule: clips ship within five working days of the event or the moment is gone. Speed beats polish for feed assets; the article is where polish lives.
- **The tracking sheet** — one row per asset: status, URL, target intent, primary metric, 90-day result. No rows, no engine.
- **The calendar slot** — repurposing is scheduled like production, not squeezed into spare afternoons. It goes on the [editorial calendar](/journal/growth/content-ops-editorial-calendar) as a standing three-day block after every event, owned by a named person. Programmes die from vague ownership before they die from anything else.

Total cost per event: roughly two to three days of combined effort for five to seven shipped assets. Anyone telling you it needs a videographer, a podcast studio and a content ops hire is selling you the department version of what should be a checklist.

## Measure per asset, not per event

"Did the webinar work?" is the wrong question — it averages a flagship article that will rank for three years with a clip that lived for nine hours. Measure each asset against its own class: the article against your article benchmarks (rankings, engaged time, assisted conversions), clips against feed benchmarks (completion rate, click-through to the article), the FAQ post against organic entrances. The tracking sheet's 90-day column is where honesty lives: most events reveal that two assets did all the work. That's fine — next event, you over-invest in those two types and cut the rest. The engine improves because the sheet exists.

Two measurement traps to refuse: counting attendees as the event's success metric (attendance measures promotion, not content), and crediting the webinar for every asset's outcomes in aggregate (which produces heroic ROI slides and zero learning). Honest per-asset numbers are what let you defend the programme when budgets tighten, because you can name exactly which outputs pay.

## The cadence that compounds

Monthly events plus a five-asset yield equals sixty assets a year from twelve hours of expert time — a publishing engine no interview programme matches, because the expert only had to show up once per cycle. We watched this pattern with podcast and community clients like [Signal & Noise](/work/signal-and-noise-podcast-network): the moment recording stopped being treated as the deliverable and started being treated as raw material, the content economics changed by an order of magnitude.

The webinar isn't the product. The webinar is the quarry.

## Key takeaways

- Plan the slices in the event brief — a target asset list shapes what the live hour captures.
- Fix a twelve-asset menu so the per-event decision is a checkbox, not a meeting.
- Map every asset to one search intent or channel and one next action; clips link to the article, never the recording.
- Keep tooling boring: transcription, one editor, a tracking sheet, a standing three-day calendar block with a named owner.
- Measure per asset against its class, review at 90 days, and let the data reshape the menu.

## FAQ

**We're B2B with small live audiences — is this still worth it?**
More so. A thirty-person webinar with the right thirty people is a focus group that also generates content. The live questions alone are worth the FAQ post, and a flagship article doesn't care how many people attended its source event. Audience size gates the *promotion* value, not the repurposing value.

**Should we gate the recording?**
Usually no. The recording's job in this system is archive hub, and a gate strangles the internal-link traffic the other assets send it. If you need the lead capture, gate something better: a guide built from three events on a theme. One gate, fed by everything.

**How do we get the expert to agree to all this slicing?**
Show them the asset list at the brief stage and the per-asset metrics after the first event. Experts hate re-recording and love reach; one 90-day review demonstrating that their Tuesday lunchtime became a ranking article and five clips converts most sceptics. Also: brief them that clean soundbites are welcome — it flatters and improves the raw material simultaneously.

**Do the written assets cannibalise the live event's SEO or vice versa?**
No — the recording page targets the event-title query; the flagship article targets the topic query; the FAQ post targets question queries. Distinct intents, one cluster. If the recording page accidentally outranks the article for the topic term, tighten the recording page's title and let the article carry the canonical weight through your [internal links](/journal/growth/internal-linking-architecture).
