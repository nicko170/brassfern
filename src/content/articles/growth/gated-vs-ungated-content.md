Somewhere in every B2B marketing team there lives a flowchart, drawn in 2016, that answers the question "should we gate this?" The flowchart says yes. The checklist beneath it says "demand gen needs MQLs this quarter," which is the true explanation, and then someone spends a sprint turning a fine PDF into a lead trap that eleven people complete, three of them competitors.

Gating is a pricing decision. You are charging an email address for a piece of content, and like every pricing decision it has two sides: what the customer pays, and what the purchase costs you. The strange thing is how rarely the second side gets counted.

## The arithmetic nobody runs

Here is the trade-off, stated flatly. An ungated asset accumulates reach: it gets indexed, linked, quoted, shared in group chats, cited in decks. A gated asset converts a slice of that reach into known contacts — and forfeits most of the rest, because every step of the funnel leaks: people who won't fill forms on principle, people on phones, people who arrived from a chat link and bail at the paywall-with-fields, search engines that can index a PDF but can't rank a landing page the way they rank a living page, and dark social, where a gated link is functionally dead.

Typical form completion on a cold content page runs a few percent. So the arithmetic of the gate is: trade potentially ten thousand readers for a few hundred addresses. That can be a good deal! But only if the address is worth more than the roughly forty-plus readers it costs. The honest version of that math:

- **Value of the address** = probability the contact is in-market × probability your nurture converts them × deal value. For most B2B content audiences, the in-market fraction is low single digits.
- **Value of the reader** = brand imprint, the link they might send to the actual buyer, the search equity, the citation, the share. Diffuse, delayed, unmeasurable — and very real.

We built an entire piece on the minefield of counting these things: [attribution that admits what it doesn't know](/journal/growth/attribution-models-honest). The short version: dark-social and brand effects resist attribution, so gate math always looks better on paper than it is in the market. Shave your spreadsheet optimism by half, then decide.

## The lead-quality myth

The argument for gating that survives scrutiny is never volume — it's quality. "The people who fill the form are the serious ones." Sometimes. More often the form filters for *tolerance of forms*, not buying intent: students, competitors, job-seekers and junior researchers complete more gates than CFOs do; the senior buyer you most want sends the link to a deputy with the words "can you download this."

The evidence we've seen across programmes, fictional-but-consistent: conversion-to-opportunity from content gates tracks the *topic's proximity to purchase*, not the existence of the gate. A pricing-guide gate outperforms a trends-report gate by an order of magnitude whether or not either has a form. Which suggests the gate is doing much less work than the topic is — and the topic was free.

Where gates genuinely do signal intent: tools and utilities with ongoing value, where the address is part of the product experience — saved calculations, benchmark reports against your own numbers, a template that gets updated. The visitor isn't paying a toll; they're creating an account-lite. That's a transaction they understand.

## A format-by-format verdict

After years of arguing about this in client workshops, our position has calcified into a table:

| Format | Verdict | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blog posts, articles, guides | **Never gate** | This is your search surface and your credibility. Gating it is burning the orchard to dry some fruit. |
| Original research & benchmark data | **Ungated summary, gated raw dataset** | The findings must travel; the workbook can charge admission. |
| Calculators, graders, configurators | **Ungated to use, gate the save** | Tools earn links like nothing else; see our [interactive-tools-as-content](/journal/growth/interactive-tools-as-content) piece for the full argument. |
| Templates & kits | **Case by case** | A genuinely load-bearing template (an RFP boilerplate, a tracking-plan sheet) earns a gate. A "5 tips checklist" does not. |
| Webinars & events | **Gate the registration, not the recording** | Registration is a real commitment signal. Gating last year's recording is nostalgia with friction. |
| Pricing and comparison content | **Never gate** | Hiding buying-stage information is self-sabotage. |

The pattern underneath: gate where the asset has *scarcity or ongoing utility*, never where its value is *spreading*. Ideas want to travel; tools and data can charge rent.

## The hybrid pattern: ungated, optional save

The pattern we now default to, and the one that usually ends the workshop argument: publish the whole thing ungated, and offer the email at the *point of delivered value* — after the reader has received something — as a convenience, not a toll. "Get this as a PDF to share with your team." "Save your benchmark and we'll email the updated dataset in Q3." "This is chapter 3 of 6 — want the rest in your inbox, one a week?"

Three properties make it work. The ask comes after value, so it reads as service rather than gatekeeping. It's optional, so reach compounds unharmed — the page ranks, links, gets read in full. And the addresses it collects are *better*, not worse: someone who read the whole thing and still wants the PDF is a warmer signal than someone who filled a form hoping the content behind the curtain was good. Completion rates are lower than a hard gate, naturally. Conversion-to-revenue from those addresses runs higher in every programme we've watched, because the form selected for gratitude instead of resignation.

Where should the ask even live? Everywhere except a modal. We've written the full case for [lead capture without the popup](/journal/growth/lead-capture-without-popups); the pattern above is its content strategy sibling.

## Rolling it back: de-gating an existing library

If you're sitting on a gated archive, the unwind is less scary than it feels. The playbook:

1. **Triage by topic-buyer distance.** Anything a searcher would want (guides, research, comparisons) de-gates first. Anything a prospect at the bottom genuinely trades for (tools, datasets) can keep the gate or move to the hybrid.
2. **Republish as real pages, not PDFs-behind-links.** Lift the content into HTML with proper internal links — this is the moment to fix the whole [content architecture](/journal/growth/content-strategy-compounds), not just unlock the door.
3. **Redirect the old gate URLs** to the new pages so link equity and bookmarks survive.
4. **Keep one honest form per asset** — the optional-save pattern — so demand gen still gets its signal. Present this to sales as upgrading lead quality, which is true, and they will eventually thank you, which is also true, if slower.

Expect a dip in raw MQL counts for a quarter and an improvement in opportunity quality shortly after. Warn the dashboard-watchers in advance so the dip is a plan, not a panic.

## Key takeaways

- Gating is pricing: you charge an email address, and the hidden cost is most of the reach, search equity and dark-social spread the asset would have earned. Run both sides of the math, then halve it.
- "Gated leads are higher quality" is mostly myth — forms filter for form tolerance. Topic proximity to purchase predicts conversion better than the gate does.
- Gate scarcity and ongoing utility (datasets, saved tools, live events); never gate things whose value is travelling — articles, research findings, pricing content.
- Default to the hybrid: fully ungated, optional save at the point of delivered value. Fewer addresses, better addresses, compounding reach.
- De-gating an archive is a triage, a republish into real HTML, a redirect map, and one honest form per asset — with a pre-announced MQL dip that comes back as pipeline quality.

## FAQ

**Our board measures marketing on MQLs. How do we de-gate without blowing up the metric?**
Change the definition before you change the forms. Propose a quarter where "MQL" includes hybrid saves AND a new engaged-reader metric (return visits on ungated assets, branded-search lift), and report pipeline per source alongside raw volume. Boards defend metrics that produce revenue; show the gate's actual opportunity rate — usually an uncomfortable number — and the redefinition becomes their idea.

**Isn't ungated content just 'brand marketing' we can't measure?**
Partially, yes — embrace the honesty. Some effects are real and resist per-asset attribution: branded search volume, direct traffic growth, "how did you hear about us" fields, sales hearing your phrases repeated on calls. Measure the portfolio against revenue over quarters rather than each asset against last week's form fills. Precise measurement of the wrong thing is how gates survived this long.

**What about gating for personalisation — 'give us your email so we can tailor the content'?**
Then tailor it, visibly, immediately. If the promised personalisation is real — a benchmark against their industry, a plan built on their answers — this is the tools-and-utilities case and the gate is legitimate. If the address goes into a newsletter drip and the "personalised" PDF is the same PDF, visitors notice the swap exactly once, and your brand absorbs the difference.

**Compliance-wise, can we email everyone who completes a gate?**
Only if they agreed to it. Separate the delivery consent ("email me the download") from marketing consent ("send me things forever"), unchecked by default, said in plain words. Bundling them inflates your list with people who will mark you as spam by Q3 — and [deliverability](/journal/growth/email-deliverability-fundamentals) is a growth lever you do not want to saw off for a vanity list number.
