Most company websites have a customer-stories page that is, functionally, a trophy shelf: eight logos, four flattering quotes, and a video nobody watches. It exists because competitors have one. It ranks for nothing, converts nobody, and updates never. Meanwhile the sales team is begging for proof, organic search is full of untapped "[problem] + [industry]" queries, and the customers themselves are sitting on stories that would close deals — if anyone asked them properly.

A customer-story programme is one of the few content investments that serves every stage of the funnel simultaneously: searchers find it, evaluators skim it, champions forward it. But only if you treat it as a programme — interview craft, structural discipline, SEO packaging, distribution, permissions — rather than as an occasional act of flattery. Here's how we run it.

## The interview: quotable numbers don't happen by accident

The difference between a story with "our check-ins dropped from 40 minutes to under 5" and a story with "it's been really positive, honestly" is decided entirely in the interview. Vague testimonials are the output of vague questions.

Our interview structure, four acts, forty minutes:

**Act one — the before, in detail.** "Walk me through the last time the old process went badly. Who was in the room? What did it cost?" You're not chasing drama; you're establishing the baseline the contrast needs. A result without a baseline is a number floating in space.

**Act two — the deciding moment.** "What almost stopped you? What did the sceptic on your team say?" Objections stated by a customer are the highest-converting material in sales enablement, because every prospect has the same sceptic in their head. This act also generates honesty that makes the story believable — perfect stories read as fiction.

**Act three — the numbers.** This is a hunt, and you hunt by narrowing: "You mentioned check-ins got faster — faster how? What was a normal Tuesday before, and what's it like now?" Then the killer follow-up: "What would you have guessed the number was, before you measured it?" People underrate their own results; the contrast between guess and measurement produces the quotes that carry case studies. When we ship client work — [Hearthbrew's retention story](/work/hearthbrew-subscription-club) or [Marlowe's direct-booking recovery](/work/marlowe-hotels-direct-booking-relaunch) — the published numbers are clearly marked illustrative; your real customer numbers deserve even more care, because they're the whole point.

**Act four — the forwardable line.** "If a peer in your industry asked whether it's worth it, what would you tell them — one sentence?" Nine in ten customers produce, on the spot, the headline quote you could never have written. Asking for one sentence is the trick: permission to be brief produces things worth quoting.

Two operational rules: record everything (transcripts are the raw mine), and never let marketing interview alone — bring the account person who knows where the bodies and the numbers are buried.

## Structure: write for the skimmer, reward the deep reader

A customer story is read three ways: skimmed in forty seconds by an evaluator, read properly by a champion building a business case, and crawled by a machine deciding whether you answer "[product category] for [industry]". The same document must serve all three.

Our template, roughly in this order:

1. **The result up front.** First screen carries the headline number and the customer's own sentence. Nobody scrolls to find out if it's worth scrolling.
2. **Pull-quote pair** — one emotional ("I stopped dreading Mondays"), one numerical ("checkout errors down 63%"), visually distinct. Skimmers read only these; make them count.
3. **Before / after**, in the customer's concrete detail from act one and act three. Challenge–approach–outcome still works — look at any story on our [work index](/work) — but anchor it to the customer's world, not the vendor's heroism. The customer is the protagonist; the product is the helpful stranger, not the hero.
4. **The objection paragraph.** What nearly stopped them, stated straight. This is the paragraph champions forward internally: "this bit is exactly Denise's concern."
5. **Specs strip** — industry, size, stack, timeline — for the crawler and the pattern-matching evaluator.
6. **The next step that matches intent.** Not "contact sales" — "read how the migration worked" or "see the pricing they'd have paid" maps to where this reader actually is.

Length target: 900–1,400 words. Long enough to rank and persuade; short enough that the champion finishes it before their meeting.

## SEO packaging: industry plus problem is the query

Customer stories rank for a distinctive query class: **[problem] in [industry]**, **[competitor] alternative**, and **"how does [company] do [thing]"**. Nobody searches "customer success story". So package accordingly:

- **Title pattern:** "[Customer/industry descriptor]: how [outcome] (the [problem] fix)". If the customer can't be named, the industry descriptor carries the keyword: "A national grocer's checkout rebuild" still ranks.
- **Headings as questions** where honest — question fragments are how evaluators actually search mid-funnel.
- **The specs strip feeds structured data**; review and article markup where appropriate, per our [schema playbook](/journal/growth/schema-markup-playbook).
- **Cluster the stories.** Stories are a natural hub: an industry hub page aggregating proof beats scattering individual stories across a blog chronology. This is [cluster thinking](/journal/growth/content-clusters-strategy) applied to the highest-intent content you own.
- **Reuse the same discipline as conversion copywriting** — specific beats impressive, plain beats clever; the [conversion copy rules](/journal/growth/conversion-copywriting) apply verbatim to headline numbers.

One honest caution: don't fabricate precision. "Down 63%" from a customer who measured it is gold. Imputing a number the customer didn't state, or averaging three vague anecdotes into a chart, is how case-study programmes end up in procurement disputes. If the numbers are soft, run the story on craft and quotes instead — a believable story outperforms a numerically impressive fiction.

## Distribution: a story is a campaign, not a URL

Publishing the page is 20% of the work. Every story we ship gets: a sales-enablement version (one page, objections highlighted, numbers in bold) added to the deal deck library; a newsletter feature — [newsletters remain the highest-trust channel](/journal/growth/newsletter-growth-engine) for this content; two or three social cuts anchored on the pull-quotes; and a slot in the nurture flow matched to the story's industry, which is exactly the kind of branching a sane [lifecycle architecture](/journal/growth/lifecycle-email-architecture) already supports. Sales should also get the recording-clip if the interview was on video: forty seconds of a real customer saying the number out loud is worth a deck of claims.

## Permissions, before you need them

The quiet killer of story programmes is the permissions mess discovered at launch: the marketing contact loves it, the legal team kills it, six weeks evaporate. Our rules: get the interview consent form signed *at booking*, covering quotes, name, logo, numbers and video, with a defined review SLA (ten working days) stated back to the customer as a promise. Offer the approval draft fast — within a week — while goodwill is warm. And keep a revocation register: customers change vendors, get acquired, rebrand. A story honouring a promised takedown in 48 hours is reputation management; a stale story discovered by the customer's new comms team is a relationship invoice come due.

## The compounding math

One story a month, done properly, is twelve pages of high-intent search surface, twelve sales assets and a newsletter year — from twelve hours of customer time. Few programmes compound like it, because every new story strengthens the hub, strengthens the proof, and makes the next customer easier to ask. The trophy shelf never did any of that.

## Key takeaways

- Quotable numbers are made in the interview: establish the before, hunt by narrowing, and always ask the one-sentence forwardable line.
- Structure for skimmers first: result on the first screen, paired pull-quotes, an objection paragraph champions can forward.
- Package for "[problem] in [industry]" queries, cluster stories on industry hubs, and never fabricate precision.
- A story is a campaign: sales one-pager, newsletter feature, social cuts, nurture-flow slot, interview clip.
- Solve permissions at booking time — consent scope, review SLA, and a revocation register you actually honour.

## FAQ

**What if customers won't be named?**
Anonymous stories still work if you keep the specifics: "a 400-store national grocer" plus real numbers beats "a leading retailer" plus vibes. Anonymity costs you the logo and the video, not the substance. And anonymised approvals are often faster — legal teams relax when their name isn't on the page.

**How do we get customers to agree in the first place?**
Ask at the moment of delivered value — after a great quarter, a successful launch — and offer something back that's real: early feature access, a co-marketing push, a charity donation, a genuinely useful benchmark report. Most refusals come from being asked at renewal time by someone they don't know.

**Static page, PDF, or both?**
The web page is canonical — it ranks, links and updates. Produce the PDF as a print stylesheet export of the same content for sales attachments; a hand-built PDF that drifts out of sync with the web version is a permissions and accuracy incident waiting to happen.

**How do we measure whether stories convert, not just rank?**
Track story-page entrances to pipeline pages (pricing, contact, demo) and tag stories in your CRM's self-reported attribution field ("how did you hear about us" plus "what content did you read"). Attribution is noisy — accept it and [make decisions anyway](/journal/growth/attribution-noise-decisions). Directional truth beats precise nonsense.
