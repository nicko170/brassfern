Somewhere on your roadmap there is probably a ticket that reads "fix accessibility" with no owner, no estimate, and a faint smell of guilt. An overlay vendor's pitch lands directly on that guilt: one line of JavaScript, a floating toolbar icon, an accessibility statement generated by lunchtime, and — the magic word — *compliance*. For a few hundred dollars a month, the ticket closes itself.

We have been brought in behind that decision a dozen times, usually after a complaint or a legal letter. This piece is what we tell the product team when we arrive: what overlays actually are, what they measurably do and don't do, why the failure is structural rather than a matter of vendor quality, and where the same budget does real work. If you want the constructive follow-through, our [WCAG AA field guide for product teams](/journal/product/wcag-aa-product-teams) and our [accessibility audit process](/journal/product/accessibility-audit-process) are the operating manuals. This one is about not buying the shortcut.

## What an overlay actually is

Strip the marketing and an overlay is a client-side script that attempts to patch your interface after render. It offers a toolbar of user-triggered adjustments (contrast toggle, font-size bump, "screen reader mode"), and it attempts automated DOM repairs: injecting missing `alt` text guesses, rewriting ARIA attributes, reshuffling tab order, patching contrast with filter hacks.

Two claims follow. First: these patches raise your conformance with WCAG. Second: the patched site will serve people with disabilities. Both claims fail, but for different reasons, and it helps to keep them separate.

## The courtroom arithmetic against the promise

The overlay industry's core promise — *install us and you won't get sued* — has been tested at scale and has failed publicly. US accessibility-filing data, tracked year over year by accessibility law practices that publish their datasets, consistently shows hundreds of businesses sued while running overlay widgets; plaintiffs' firms now actively target overlay users, because the toolbar is a machine-detectable admission that the underlying site was never accessible. Industry trackers counted over a quarter of recent-year digital accessibility lawsuits naming companies that had an overlay installed. The widget is not a shield. It is a flag.

Regulators have noticed too. The US Federal Trade Commission reached a consent order with the largest overlay vendor over misleading claims. A coalition of several hundred accessibility professionals and disabled users signed a public statement urging organisations to stop using overlays at all. Community-built browser extensions exist whose sole purpose is to block overlay scripts from running, because some disabled users find the "help" actively breaks their setup. (All of this describes the category, not one bad apple — the pattern holds across vendors.)

None of that litigation detail is the real argument, though. The real argument is that overlays don't work as software. That's the part you can verify yourself, in an afternoon, from your desk.

## Five things overlays cannot fix

When we audit a product that has an overlay running, we flip it off and run our normal process. Here is what is always still broken underneath:

1. **Focus order and keyboard flow through real components.** A modal that opens without focus entering it, a dropdown that traps Tab, a wizard where focus resets to the top of the page on every step — these are structural behaviours written into your components. A script that rewrites attributes cannot re-choreograph component state machines it doesn't understand.
2. **Meaningful labels for your domain's UI.** Automated alt-text injection produces descriptions like "image" or a guess at dominant colours, because that is what the script can compute. It cannot know that the chart is the pricing comparison the user came for. The semantics of *your* product require someone who knows *your* product.
3. **Error identification and recovery.** When a form field fails validation, screen reader users need the error announced, associated with the field, and phrased usefully. Overlays patch static attributes; they do not intercept your validation lifecycle and make it sensible to assistive tech. (Our piece on [error messages that de-escalate](/journal/product/error-messages-that-help) is the fix; it's work.)
4. **Anything dynamic.** Live region announcements for async updates, drag-and-drop alternatives, infinite scroll, a canvas-based visualiser — the parts of modern products that move are the parts overlays can't see coming.
5. **Your PDFs, your emails, your checkout provider's iframe.** Conformance covers the experience, not the viewport your script can reach.

Contrast-reskin features deserve a special mention, because they are the most visible overlay feature and the most harmful: overriding colours with CSS filters routinely inverts charts into nonsense, breaks brand-legible UI, and stomps on settings users have already configured in their OS. Users who need high contrast mostly *already have it*, at the operating system level, configured to their needs. Your toolbar toggle is a worse version of a tool they already own.

## Why the failure is structural

It would be tidy if this were a story about one vendor cutting corners. It isn't. The overlay model is wrong in principle, for one reason: **accessibility is a property of the interface as designed, and patches are applied to an interface as guessed.**

An overlay must infer from the DOM what your components mean. But meaning lives upstream — in design decisions, in component contracts, in content authors' choices, in the state machines of your flows. A script that sees a `<div>` with an onClick cannot know it should have been a button with keyboard handlers, a visible label, and a place in the tab order; it can only glue attributes onto the div and hope. We regularly see overlay "repairs" make things measurably worse: ARIA roles stapled onto elements whose behaviour doesn't match the role (a `role="button"` that still can't be keyboard-activated is a lie told in a formal language), tab order rewritten into a spiral, headings fabricated out of div soup in an order that scrambles the page structure actual screen-reader users rely on.

WCAG conformance is measured against the experience. Assistive technology users run their own stack — JAWS, NVDA, VoiceOver, ZoomText, switch devices — tuned over years. The overlay's "screen reader mode" is a parallel, inferior, generic stack competing with the expert stack the user brought. This is why disabled communities' response to overlays is not indifference. It is active hostility.

## How to audit a vendor claim in one meeting

When the renewal comes around, or a new vendor pitches, this is the twenty-minute protocol we give clients:

- **Ask for the conformance claim in writing.** "WCAG 2.2 AA conformant after installation" is the magic phrase. Vendors who phrase it as "helps you towards WCAG" are telling you the truth quietly.
- **Ask how the fix survives what the overlay can't see.** Shadow DOM components, third-party iframes, dynamically injected content after user interaction. Watch for the pivot to "our AI handles it."
- **Run their demo site with a screen reader for ten minutes.** NVDA is free. If the vendor's own showcase is rough, the answer is in.
- **Ask for references from disabled users, not from other customers.** Customers bought the same promise you were pitched.
- **Cost out the honest alternative on the same slide.** Put the annual overlay fee next to an audit plus a remediation retainer. The honest option is usually the same order of magnitude — it just doesn't pretend to be instant.

## Where the budget actually goes

The redirect is a three-step ladder, and it's the same one we run as part of our [product design and engineering](/services/product) engagements:

**Step one: a real audit (weeks, not months).** Automated scanning (axe and friends) covers maybe a third of WCAG reliably, so the audit must be manual-assisted: keyboard passes, screen reader passes, zoom and reflow passes, run against your core flows, not your page count. Output: an issue list ranked by severity and flow criticality, each item with reproduction and a pointer to the owning component. This is the artefact the overlay vendor convinced you that you didn't need.

**Step two: fix the design system, then the flows.** Most findings trace to a handful of shared components — the modal, the form field, the menu, the toast. Fix them once, at the source, and whole categories of failure disappear product-wide. This is where overlay economics invert completely: the overlay bills you monthly forever to *not* fix components you could repair in a sprint.

**Step three: make it a gate, not a mood.** Accessibility checks in CI for the automatable slice, a keyboard smoke path in your release checklist, and a standing line in the definition of done. Accessibility that depends on quarterly enthusiasm decays; accessibility wired into the pipeline compounds.

Retire the overlay the day step two lands its first release. Keep a copy of your audit and remediation log — it's both good engineering and the only legal posture that has ever meant anything: demonstrable, ongoing effort.

## The uncomfortable reason overlays sell

Overlays persist because they let a company perform accessibility in the cheapest available currency — a procurement decision — instead of paying for it in the only currency that works, which is engineering and design attention. The toolbar icon is legible to an executive; a corrected focus trap is not. Once you see the purchase as a communication to leadership rather than a service to users, the category's survival makes perfect sense.

The way through is to make the real work legible too. A one-page audit summary with counts by severity gets read. A burn-down chart of remediated issues gets read. A fifteen-minute session where an executive watches a screen reader user attempt the checkout in question gets *remembered*. That last one, run once, has ended every overlay conversation we've ever been part of.

## Key takeaways

- Overlays are post-render DOM patches. They cannot repair component behaviour, domain semantics, error flows, dynamic content, or anything outside the script's reach — which is most of what WCAG actually measures.
- The legal promise is inverted: overlay users appear disproportionately in accessibility lawsuits, regulators have acted on misleading claims, and the toolbar advertises that the site underneath was never fixed.
- The failure is structural, not vendor-specific: accessibility lives in design decisions and component contracts, upstream of anything a script can infer.
- Audit vendor claims with four questions: written conformance claim, dynamic-content handling, screen-reader demo, and references from disabled users — then cost the honest alternative on the same slide.
- Redirect the same budget: real audit, fix the shared components, wire checks into CI. It costs the same order of money and actually compounds.
- Make real work legible to leadership — severity dashboards and one watched user session outperform any toolbar icon.

## FAQ

**Is there anything overlay-adjacent that's legitimate?**
User-triggered display preferences are legitimate — when they're your own: a contrast theme you designed and tested, font-size controls that respect your layout. The line is whether *you* own the behaviour and it was *built for your interface*. A generic script guessing at your DOM is the problem; authored preferences are a feature.

**We have a legal deadline in sixty days. Isn't an overlay better than nothing?**
No, and it's worse than a partial honest fix: it adds new failure surfaces on top of the old ones and signals no remediation happened. With sixty days, run the audit and fix the top-severity flow issues (usually: keyboard traps, form errors, unlabeled controls). Courts and regulators respond to documented effort; they do not respond to widgets.

**How do automated scanners fit in — if not overlays, just axe in CI?**
Scanners are step one of the honest path, not the whole path. They reliably catch a minority of issues (missing labels, contrast values, malformed ARIA) and are blind to the majority (focus choreography, meaningful text, interaction logic). CI scanning is cheap and worth it; it's a floor, not a ceiling.

**Our overlay vendor claims their AI now understands page semantics. Does that change the analysis?**
Language models can generate more plausible alt text than a 2019 heuristic — and plausible is precisely the trap. A fluent guess about your pricing chart is still a guess, delivered with a confidence your users can't audit. Until the model can attend your design reviews, semantics belong to people who know what the interface is for.

**What should our accessibility statement say once we're on the honest path?**
State what conformant means for you (WCAG 2.2 AA, scoped), what you've audited and when, known limitations with remediation dates, and a real contact channel with a service-level commitment. A statement written from your audit report, updated quarterly, is both more credible and more defensible than any badge a vendor can sell you.
