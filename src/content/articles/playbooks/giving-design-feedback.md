Here is a sentence that has cost the design industry millions of hours: "I'll know it when I see it." It feels like a high standard. It's actually an abdication — it transfers the entire intellectual work of defining success onto the design team, who must now generate options until one of them trips an unarticulated wire in the reviewer's head. Designers can't aim at a target that crystallises only when hit. They will, however, bill for every shot.

Good design feedback is a skill, and like most skills it has learnable mechanics. Clients who have it get better work faster for less money, because every feedback round moves downhill instead of sideways. This is the method we teach at the start of every engagement — and what follows is written for the client side of the table, though designers reading it will recognise the mirror of our own [critique method](/journal/web-design/design-critique-method).

## The one question that does half the work

Before offering any reaction, ask: **what problem is this solving?** Not rhetorically — actually ask, and listen to the answer. The response tells you which of two worlds you're in.

If the designer has a crisp answer — "the comparison table is designed for the 60% of your traffic that arrives from competitor-alternative searches; that's why the pricing row is sticky" — then your feedback should engage with that reasoning: do you agree the problem is the right one? Is the reasoning about your customers right? This conversation is fast and high-value.

If the designer can't answer, you've caught something more valuable than any colour correction: decoration masquerading as decision. Now the feedback writes itself. "What problem is this solving?" asked kindly and consistently is the single highest-leverage move a client owns. It also signals, within about two rounds, that you expect reasoning — which raises the quality of everything you see, because designers prepare differently for reviewers who ask why.

## Reaction versus direction: know which one you're giving

Feedback comes in two species and they must never be confused.

**Reaction** is diagnostic data: "I had to read this headline twice," "this page feels frantic," "I didn't notice the pricing until you pointed at it." Reactions are always legitimate and often gold — you're reporting what the design does to a real human with real context about the business. You can't be wrong about your own reaction.

**Direction** is a proposed solution: "make the logo bigger," "put the form above the fold," "use the blue from our 2019 campaign." Direction carries an implicit claim that you know the solution space, and you might — but the claim needs evidence.

The failure mode is direction disguised as reaction. "It doesn't feel premium" is useful. "It doesn't feel premium" followed by "so make the type serif and add gold" skips the entire diagnosis: *what* isn't premium — the type, the spacing, the image quality, the copy's vocabulary? Each has a different fix, and four of the five have nothing to do with serifs.

The discipline: say the reaction, describe the gap, then stop. "This doesn't feel premium — the photography reads stock and there's no air around anything." A strong design team will return with three ways to close that gap, two of which you'd never have thought to ask for. That's what you're paying for. If you catch yourself dictating solutions, check whether you're actually just describing the missing thing — and if you genuinely know the solution, say why, and label it as a constraint rather than an instruction.

## Consolidate the voices before they arrive

The most destructive feedback pattern isn't harsh — it's plural. Seven stakeholders reply independently to the design link; the designer receives a spreadsheet of contradictions; the next round is a compromise smoothie; everyone hates it equally; round four begins.

The fix belongs to the client and takes one discipline: **feedback arrives through one throat.** Collect your stakeholders' input internally, resolve the contradictions yourselves, and deliver one consolidated position. Where your organisation genuinely can't resolve a contradiction — legal wants the disclaimer prominent, marketing wants it invisible — that's a decision for the named approver on the [decision map](/journal/playbooks/stakeholder-alignment-design), not a note for the designer.

Consolidation also means deduplicating observation from preference. When three stakeholders say the testimonial section felt untrustworthy and one says he prefers blue links, those are not four equivalent items. The first is a finding; the second is a preference; your consolidated feedback reflects the difference.

## Hierarchy in every round: blocking, important, polish

Even consolidated feedback fails if it's flat. Forty comments with no priority forces the designer to guess which ones you'd trade away — and designers guess wrong, spending the revision budget on comment 34 when comment 2 was the one keeping you up at night.

Tag every item in one of three tiers:

- **Blocking** — must change before this can proceed. Keep this list brutally short; a blocking list with twelve items is a crying list.
- **Important** — should change in a normal round, but you'd ship without it at a pinch.
- **Polish** — noted for completeness; fit if cheap.

The tiers do two things: they let the team sequence honestly ("we can close all your blocking items this week, important by next demo, polish goes to the backlog"), and they force *you* to discover that the thing you were about to go to war over was, on reflection, an important at best. Clients who tier their feedback report a strange experience: projects feel calmer and faster without any actual change in pace. That's what removing invisible priority confusion feels like.

## Time in the file beats meetings about the file

Annotated comments on the work itself — the design file, the staging build — beat meeting-based feedback in every measurable way: precision, speed, and the quality of what comes back. A comment pinned to the element ("this heading") outperforms "the second thing on the screenshare" by exactly the distance between pointing and describing.

Meetings still matter for one thing: the *why* conversations. When a blocking item is really a strategy disagreement wearing a design costume — you're not disputing the layout, you're disputing whether the pricing page should lead with annual plans — no amount of comment threads will resolve it. Name it, take it to a short call with the approver, and don't let the design round stall waiting for it. The [weekly note format](/journal/playbooks/status-updates-clients-read) has a "decisions needed" section precisely so these surface instead of festering.

## Three scripts that keep rounds fast

Some phrases reliably accelerate feedback rounds. Steal them:

1. **"Here's the reaction; the solution is yours."** — For when you know what's wrong but not the fix. Grants the team license to solve it properly.
2. **"If we could only change one thing, it's this."** — Forces hierarchy, guarantees the next round starts with the load-bearing item.
3. **"This is the last large round; from here we're polishing."** — Names the phase. Projects without phase names get infinite "one more thought" rounds, because nobody has announced that the thought window is closing.

And one to avoid at all costs: "just make it pop." Every designer who hears it translates it the same way: *the reviewer has a real reaction they haven't yet found the words for.* Find the words. "It doesn't stand out against the sections around it" is actionable; "pop" is a shrug in a blazer.

## When you genuinely don't like it

Sometimes the honest feedback is "this direction is wrong," and it should be said exactly that plainly, early. The rescue format: name what's working first (be genuine — something almost always is), state precisely what feels wrong and against which goal, and confirm the timeline consequence out loud. "The editorial warmth is right; the product pages feel like a brochure site, and our goal was a tool people trust with their books — I think the direction needs a hard turn. What does a turn cost at this stage?" Asked in week three, that conversation costs a week. In week ten, ask a [studio about their process](/approach) and you'll hear the wince. Direction corrections get exponentially more expensive; candour is the discount.

## Key takeaways

- Open with "what problem is this solving?" A crisp answer means you're debating reasoning; no answer means you've caught decoration.
- Distinguish reactions (always valid, always useful) from directions (need evidence, often better left to the team). Diagnose the gap; prescribe sparingly.
- Consolidate stakeholder voices into one position before the design team hears any of them. Contradictions are resolved inside your house or paid for in rounds.
- Tier every comment: blocking / important / polish. Flat feedback forces the team to guess your priorities, and they will guess wrong.
- Annotate the work, meet about the why. Strategy disagreements wearing design costumes go to the approver, not the comment thread.
- "This direction is wrong" is legitimate feedback — cheapest in week three, expensive in week ten. Plainly, early, and with the cost conversation attached.

## FAQ

**What if I genuinely can't articulate why something feels off?**
Report the reaction without the analysis — "I've looked at this six times and something about the hero makes me want to leave" — and give the team permission to diagnose. An honest unarticulated reaction is data; a fake reason ("it's the kerning") sends designers fixing things that were fine. Also try the comparison trick: name two sites, one that has the feeling and one that doesn't. That pair usually contains the answer.

**Our CEO gives feedback directly to designers and contradicts everything we agreed. Help?**
That's a governance problem, not a feedback problem, and it won't be fixed by better phrasing. Route every voice through the one throat, and get the CEO a designated moment on the decision map — as covered in [aligning stakeholders](/journal/playbooks/stakeholder-alignment-design) — so their authority is honoured on schedule rather than exercised in corridors.

**Is "I love it, ship it" useful feedback?**
Surprisingly, yes — if it's specific about what landed. "Ship it" alone loses the chance to bank what worked. "The step-by-step disclosure of pricing is exactly the honesty our brand needed; the mobile number formatting sold it" teaches the team which instincts to repeat, which matters for every round after.

**How many feedback rounds should a healthy project need?**
On a well-run engagement: two rounds to direction-lock, one more to refine, then polish. If you're in round six on the same screen, something upstream is broken — the brief, the decision map, or the problem definition — and another round won't fix it. Name the stall instead of scheduling round seven.
