Every company has an employer brand. The only choice is whether it was written by the people who do the work or by the people who fear the resignation letter. You can tell which one you're reading in the first paragraph: "fast-paced environment" and "like a family" mean the latter; a sentence a real engineer would say out loud in a pub means the former. Candidates can smell the difference the way editors smell a press release — and the candidates you actually want are the best smellers.

The employer brand isn't a marketing problem to be solved on the careers page. It's the gap (or, gloriously, the absence of a gap) between the story you tell hires and the Tuesday your team actually has. Close that gap, and the recruiting copy writes itself. Here's how we advise clients on the writing and design layer — and how we hold ourselves [to the same standard](/careers).

## The myth of the perks page

Somewhere around 2015, employer brand became a list of objects: the ping pong table, the kombucha tap, the "wellness allowance", the office dog with a job title. The perks page was supposed to signal culture. What it signalled was that nobody could articulate the culture, so they photographed the furniture.

Two problems. First, perks are copyable — every rival can match the allowance by next quarter, which makes perks the commodity layer rather than the identity layer. Second, the candidates perks attract are selecting for perks, and they're the first to leave when a shinier allowance appears.

The things strong candidates actually screen for are un-copyable because they're structural:

- **Who decides?** Squad autonomy with named humans, or seven approvers on a colour?
- **What shipped lately?** Live work with real URLs, not a reel of mood boards.
- **What does a bad week look like here?** Every studio has them. The honest one states it.
- **What's the deal, exactly?** Salary, hours, remote policy, progression — in numbers, not adjectives.

Your employer brand story is whatever the honest answers to those questions sound like in your voice. Everything else is set dressing.

## The receipts audit

Before writing a word of recruiting content, we run what we call the receipts audit: every claim on the careers page must be backed by evidence a candidate can check from their sofa.

| Claim | Receipt |
| --- | --- |
| "We ship fast" | A public changelog, launch dates on the [work page](/work), weekly-demo culture in writing |
| "You will grow here" | Named people, hired as X, now doing Y, willing to say so on record |
| "Work–life balance is real" | Actual response-time norms, stated meeting-load caps, a logout-time culture someone senior models |
| "We're serious about craft" | The [approach page](/approach) with actual methods, not process-stock-iconography |

Two things fall out of the audit immediately. One: most careers page claims have no receipts, and get deleted rather than reworded — deletion is the edit that makes the page believable. Two: the remaining claims are usually *better* than the fog that replaced them. "You'll demo your work to the client every Friday at 3pm, and our producer will have already sent the agenda" is a stronger sentence than "fast-paced, collaborative environment". It's a picture.

A claim without a receipt isn't neutral; it costs trust. Candidates assume (correctly) that confident unverifiable claims are the inverse of the truth.

## Salary transparency: the scariest, cheapest differentiator

Putting numbers in job ads remains the fastest way to stand out, because most companies still won't. The anxiety is real — bands create internal conversations — but let's be honest about the trade:

- Hiding the number selects against the exact candidates you want. Senior people don't apply to mysteries; people with caring responsibilities and limited hours can't gamble a three-week process on a number.
- The "we'd rather discuss" negotiation frame is a power move candidates have collectively learned to dislike, and it disproportionately costs you applicants who've been underpaid before — which is to say, it filters out diversity you claim to want.
- Publishing the band *simplifies everything downstream*. No offer-stage disappointments, no churn from discoverable inequity, and a permanent public proof that your internal equity is real enough to show.

The standard we write toward: a salary band in the ad, the remote/hybrid reality named ("Sydney studio, two anchor days, remote-tolerant" beats "flexible!" which means nothing), and the response promise kept ("every applicant hears back within two weeks" — then staffing the inbox so it's true). These are brand behaviours, and they're remembered and recounted in every community your future candidates live in.

## Showing real work

Nobody believes employer brand content about work. They believe work. The strongest assets a hiring page can carry are boringly specific: a case study by the person who did it, in their words, with their name attached. "How Aiko rebuilt onboarding in six weeks" beats "Exciting challenges await!" by an unbridgeable margin.

Do this deliberately:

1. **Attach names to shipped work.** Ship case studies authored by the humans involved — we do this with bylines on [our journal](/journal) and team pages, and every candidate click-path ends at a real person with a real practice.
2. **Write job ads in your actual voice.** If the [brand voice](/journal/brand/brand-voice-survives-handover) guidelines say "confident, witty, warm, precise", the job ad is a flagship application of it — not the place voice goes to die under HR boilerplate. A job ad the team would forward to a friend ("you should apply for this, it literally describes you") is the entire KPI.
3. **Show the machinery, not the mythology.** Photos of the crit wall mid-argument, the estimate spreadsheet, the post-launch retro notes (anonymised where needed). Process artefacts are the highest-trust content in recruiting because they're unglamorous.
4. **Let leavers speak.** The uncomfortable superpower: maintain warm relationships with alumni and let candidates talk to them. An ex-employee who'll say "loved it, grew out of it, still recommend it" is worth a hundred glassdoor-paranoia countermeasures.

And the anti-pattern to ban: the fake-candid office photoshoot. Stock-adjacent imagery of beautiful people pointing at whiteboards reads as what it is — the company hiring actors instead of shipping.

## Where employer brand actually lives

Not on the careers page. The careers page is the summary; the employer brand lives everywhere the practice is visible — the journal with bylines, the approach page with honest methods, the changelog, the 404 with personality, the way the website itself is built. Every serious candidate views-source you.

Which means employer brand is inseparable from the brand proper. A company whose public writing is sharp and whose recruiting copy is sludge has told candidates something true and bad: the craft is reserved for paying customers. [Founder-led brands](/journal/brand/founder-led-brand) face this acutely — the founder's voice *is* the employer brand until the team's voices are deliberately amplified, and the handover has to be designed, not assumed.

The closing test we run on every careers page we write: read it aloud to the team, behind closed doors, and ask one question — "would you forward this?" Not "is it accurate" (accuracy is the floor). Would you forward it to the friend whose judgement you'd stake your reputation on? If the room laughs at a sentence, the sentence is lying. Cut it until the room goes quiet and someone says "yeah, that's us."

## Key takeaways

- Employer brand is the gap between the hiring story and the studio floor. Close the gap first; write second.
- Run a receipts audit: every claim needs checkable evidence, and unverifiable claims cost more trust than silence.
- Publish salary bands, remote realities and response promises in the ad. Transparency is the cheapest differentiator left.
- Show real work with real names attached; process artefacts beat culture photography.
- The employer brand lives in every public surface. Candidates read the 404 before the benefits list.
- The only approval test that matters: would the team forward it?

## FAQ

**How do we do employer brand without embarrassing the team?**
Ask them. Hire a facilitator (or be one), run the receipts audit as a workshop, and let people veto anything written about their work. Teams don't hate being shown off; they hate being misdescribed. Co-authorship is the difference.

**Isn't salary transparency just an invitation for poaching?**
Rivals already know roughly what you pay — recruiters tell them weekly. The people genuinely in the dark are your candidates and your juniors, and darkness serves neither. The poaching risk of a band is a rounding error against the trust it buys.

**We're small. Can we compete on employer brand at all?**
Small is the argument. Ten-person studios can promise things the enterprise can't fit in a spreadsheet: direct access to the work, titles that mean something, a known name in every client meeting. Write *that*, with receipts. The candidate choosing between you and a corporation isn't comparing perks; they're comparing Tuesdays. Our [first-thirty-days onboarding playbook](/journal/playbooks/agency-onboarding-first-30-days) shows the texture candidates actually ask about.

**How often should careers content be updated?**
Every time reality changes — team size, tools, policies, clients. A careers page mentioning a departed team member or a sunsetted tech stack is a brand behaviour too, and it says: we don't sweep our own floors. Quarterly review, owner named, fifteen minutes.
