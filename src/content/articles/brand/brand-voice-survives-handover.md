Every voice project we've ever run had the same funeral, held about four months after launch. The brand team loved the document. The launch copy sang. Then the deck got filed, the champion moved teams, and a paid-ads contractor wrote "Leverage best-in-class solutions for your growth journey" over a picture of a handshake. The voice didn't fail — the *handover* failed.

This piece is about that gap: not how to *find* a voice (we've written about [teaching tone with charts](/journal/brand/brand-voice-charts)), but how to build one that keeps working when the people who made it are no longer in the room. Because they never are. Voice only exists in the mouths of whoever writes next.

## Write for the reader in a hurry

The honest mental model for your guidelines' user: a marketing manager at 4:40pm on a Wednesday, three quarter-way through six tasks, about to write a launch email. They will not read your forty pages. They will read what loads fastest and answers the question in front of them.

So we structure every handover document the way that person actually works:

1. **The 90-second page.** Front of the document, printable, genuinely one page: the three principles, the five-axis chart, the top five dos and don'ts, and one before/after pair. If someone reads nothing else, this page alone should stop the worst offences.
2. **The situation index.** Not "voice theory" but *what am I writing* — launch email, apology, pricing page, app-store reply, investor update. Each gets its own short page with one example, one anti-example, one rule that matters for that context.
3. **The deep reference.** The full rationale, the grammar policy, the full rewrite tables. Nobody reads this until they disagree with something — which is exactly when it needs to exist.

The test of the structure isn't whether a strategist finds it elegant. It's whether the rushed reader finds their page. We watch new hires use the document for a week before we call it done — the same live-fire instinct behind our [living guidelines](/journal/brand/brand-guidelines-living) work.

## Decisions beat principles, under load

"Be warm but not folksy" is a principle. Under deadline pressure, principles evaporate and habits take over. What survives pressure is a *decision already made*:

- Contractions, always. (Decided. Move on.)
- We never use the user's own data back at them in marketing copy. (Decided.)
- Exclamation marks: one per email, never in product UI, never in bad news. (Decided.)
- "Please" appears in requests for user effort — restarting, checking a setting — and never in legal cover-ourselves sentences.

A guidelines doc is, honestly, a cache of pre-made decisions so the writer doesn't have to think when there's no time to think. Every decision the document *doesn't* make gets made at 4:40pm by whoever's holding the keyboard, and their answer will be whatever their last company did. When we rebuilt the voice for [Copperline Mutual](/work/copperline-community-bank), the single most-used page wasn't the philosophy — it was a list of forty of these rulings, alphabetised. "Can we say 'funds' or do we say 'money'?" Money. Decided.

## The 15-minute voice test

Here's the tool we leave behind, and the one clients still mention years later. Once a month, whoever owns voice runs the test:

1. Pull five things the organisation published this month — chosen *randomly*, not curated. A social post, a support reply, a product string, a sales email, whatever a random number generator hands you.
2. Score each against the axes: where does it sit versus where it should? One diagnosis per piece, one sentence each.
3. Total time: fifteen minutes. Output: a five-row doc in the shared channel.

Three properties make this work where audits fail. It's **random**, so teams can't polish a showcase sample. It's **small**, so it actually happens — nobody schedules a quarterly audit they dread, but fifteen minutes fits between meetings. And it's **public but kind**: the point is calibrating the instrument, not shaming the writer. Diagnose the copy, never the person.

Six months of 15-minute tests teach a team more than any document, because the document gets cited monthly in context. The chart stops being a poster and becomes a habit. We stole the pre-registration instinct from our growth colleagues' [experiment design](/journal/growth/cro-experiment-design) — decide how you'll measure before you look, then look briefly and often.

## Give the voice an owner with actual authority

A voice with no owner is a rumour. But the wrong owner is worse than none: we've seen voice handed to the most senior marketer (no time), the most junior copywriter (no authority), and "the brand guild" (a meeting that produces minutes, not rulings).

The owner needs exactly three things:

- **Time**: a standing slice of their week — for a mid-size company, half a day — protected like a release window.
- **Authority**: the right to say "this ships over my objection" and have it matter, granted explicitly by whoever signs off marketing.
- **A succession plan**: voice ownership written into the role description, not the person. Champions leave; roles persist.

Title matters less than you'd think. The best voice owner we ever worked with was a support-lead-turned-content-designer who simply cared and was allowed to. The worst was a celebrated creative director who treated every ruling as a chance to re-litigate the brand.

## Onboard the people who write the most, not the most visible

Every handover plan we've audited had a workshop for marketing and nothing for the two groups who write the majority of words customers read: support and sales. Support macros alone can out-volume the entire marketing site in a quarter. If the macros still sound like a tribunal, your rebrand is a hat on a goat. (We've written separately about the craft of [error and service copy](/journal/product/error-messages-that-help) — it deserves real budget.)

The fix is boring: the situation index needs pages for *their* contexts, written from *their* real tickets, and someone from each of those teams in the calibration loop. When [Hearthbrew's](/work/hearthbrew-subscription-club) subscription pause-email went from "Your subscription has been suspended at your request" to "Paused, no worries — we'll skip your next box and pick up whenever you're ready," support tickets about pausing *dropped*, because the voice finally carried the policy instead of hiding it.

## What to measure, if you must measure

We're wary of voice metrics — most are [vanity in a lab coat](/journal/brand/measuring-brand-health). Three that survive scrutiny: comprehension testing on key service copy (can a first-time reader say what they'll be charged, and when?), support-contact rate on voice-heavy moments (renewals, price rises, apologies), and the 15-minute test's drift score over time. If drift narrows quarter over quarter, the handover is holding.

That's the whole game, really. A voice document is finished not when it's beautiful but when a stranger, in a hurry, on a bad day, writes something that sounds like you.

## Key takeaways

- Write the doc for the 4:40pm reader: a 90-second page, a situation index, then the deep reference.
- Decisions survive deadline pressure; principles don't. Pre-make forty small rulings and alphabetise them.
- Run the monthly 15-minute voice test: five random published pieces, scored against the axes, shared kindly.
- Assign one owner with protected time, real authority, and a succession plan written into the role.
- Onboard support and sales first — they write more customer-facing words than marketing ever will.
- Measure comprehension, contact rate on voice-heavy moments, and drift over time; ignore the rest.

## FAQ

### How long should brand voice guidelines be?

As long as they need to be and as short as possible — which usually means one page of essentials, six to ten situation pages, and a reference section nobody's forced to read. If the essentials don't fit on one page, you haven't decided what's essential. Length is fine; *unfindability* is fatal.

### Who should own brand voice after the agency leaves?

One named human with time, authority and a succession plan — usually a senior writer or content designer embedded in the team that publishes most. Not a committee, not the CMO, and not the agency on retainer. We hand over rulings authority deliberately, because a voice that needs its makers' permission is already dead.

### How do you stop AI writing tools diluting the voice?

Feed the machine the same handover the humans get: the 90-second page, the rulings list, ten before/after pairs, pasted into the prompt or the tool's style settings. Then extend the 15-minute voice test to a few AI-drafted pieces each month. The teams that struggle banned the tools and lost; the ones that calibrated and spot-checked kept their voice *and* the speed.

### What's the first sign a handover is failing?

The 15-minute test catches it earliest: scores drift toward a different company — usually the writers' previous employer's voice. You'll also see the situation index pages with the fewest views matching the contexts with the most complaints. Drift is normal; unnoticed drift is the failure mode.
