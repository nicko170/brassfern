Analytics have a way of telling on you. Ask a company to rank its pages by importance and Careers comes in ninth, behind a blog post from 2021. Ask analytics to rank pages by *engaged reading time* from high-intent visitors, and Careers is top three, every time, without exception. Candidates read it for twenty minutes before applying. Clients read it before signing, because team quality is delivery quality. Journalists read it. Investors read it.

And what they find, at most companies, is the one page that skipped the rebrand: a stock photo of laughing colleagues who don't work there, a mission statement in the passive aggressive voice, and a list of perks ("free fruit!") assembled by committee. The careers page is the most-read brand page nobody designed — and fixing it is one of the highest-leverage brand investments available, because the audience arrives pre-persuaded and primed to believe.

Here's how we approach employer brand work, and the specific decisions that separate a careers page that recruits from one that merely exists. Much of it is the same discipline as any [brand identity](/services/brand-identity) project — applied inward.

## Voice parity: the rule above all rules

If your customer-facing brand is warm, sharp and specific, and your careers copy reads like a compromise between three departments, candidates will believe the worse document. They should. The careers page is the only place where a company describes itself *unmediated by a product*, and readers correctly treat it as the unguarded version.

Voice parity means the careers page is written in your actual brand voice, by whoever owns it — not in "HR professional," which is a dialect nobody speaks socially. When we rebuilt careers content for clients, the test is a read-aloud swap: lift a paragraph from the careers page onto the homepage and see if anyone notices. If it clangs, rewrite. The mechanics of a voice that survives multiple authors are in [voice charts: making tone teachable](/journal/brand/brand-voice-charts) and [brand voice that survives the handover](/journal/brand/brand-voice-survives-handover) — the same system, pointed at recruitment copy.

A few tone traps specific to careers content deserve naming:

- **"We're like a family."** You're not, and candidates have learned to read this as a warning about boundaries. Describe the actual social texture: how decisions get made, how disagreement works, what a hard week looks like. Specificity is what family-speak is avoiding; that's why it works.
- **Perk-listing as culture.** Free fruit is an amenity, not a value. If perks appear at all, they follow the principles — and the interesting ones are structural: how leave actually works, what the on-call rota feels like, how parental leave is genuinely taken (not just offered).
- **Heroic suffering.** Phrases like "we move fast and break things" have curdled into a genre candidates filter out. Replace velocity claims with evidence: your deploy cadence, your decision-making speed, a real example of something shipped in a week.

## The photography decision: real, illustrated, or nothing

Careers pages live or die on imagery, and the imagery question divides into three defensible policies — and one indefensible default (stock photos of not-your-people, which readers now identify in under a second, and which costs you precisely the trust the page exists to build).

**Real photography, honestly executed.** The strongest option if you can sustain it. The rules that make it work: shoot your actual team doing actual work (candids beat portraits; meetings beat paddle-boarding away-days); include the unphotogenic reality of your offices rather than the corner you cleaned; refresh annually, because departure-honest photography — people who've since left, still smiling on the careers page — is a specific betrayal candidates discover in their first week. Budget the real cost: a proper shoot day, plus consent and usage agreements, every year, forever.

**Illustration.** Our own honest preference at Brassfern: every portrait on [our team page](/team) is a stylised illustration, not a photograph. It solves consent, turnover, remote-distribution and consistency in one decision, and it signals something true — that you're a design-led company comfortable with artifice acknowledged as artifice. The trade-off is a colder first impression, which you buy back with specificity everywhere else.

**Nothing.** The underrated option. A type-led careers page with no people imagery at all — roles, writing samples, the actual work — outperforms a badly photographed one. Absence reads as confidence; bad photography reads as franchise.

Pick a policy and write it down. The failure mode isn't choosing wrong; it's the mixed page — one real photo from 2023, two stock images, and an illustration a contractor added — which communicates that nobody is in charge of anything.

## Salary transparency: present it or explain it

The candidates you most want have learned to read salary opacity as a range of bad hypotheses. Publishing bands is increasingly the norm in AU/NZ and London markets, and every year the cost of silence rises. So the careers page decision is binary: publish, or explain why you don't — but silence is no longer neutral, and treating it as neutral is how strong candidates self-select out before your recruiting team knows they existed.

If you publish, present properly:

- **Bands per level, per location,** with your level definitions linked. A naked number invites misreading; a band inside a levelling framework invites calibration.
- **The total-comp picture.** Equity terms in plain language, super/pension, leave, parental policy, the on-call deal. Candidates do the maths; give them good inputs.
- **Review cadence.** When do bands move, and by what process? This one line does more for trust than the number itself.

If you don't publish, say why in one honest sentence — "bands are set per engagement and we're not ready to publish them" lands better than silence — and commit to a date. Either way, the careers page that engages the question head-on outperforms the one that hopes nobody asks. They ask.

## Show the work, not the workshop

Candidates evaluate two things: the people and the problems. We've covered imagery; the problems are the bigger miss on most careers pages. "Work on exciting challenges" is wallpaper. The alternative is cheap and devastatingly effective: **show actual work.**

Link to the case studies and shipped work the team is publicly proud of — our own [work index](/work) does double duty, and that's the point, because the audience for "look what we built" and "come build with us" overlaps almost completely. Write two paragraphs per discipline on what the craft bar looks like here: what a good design critique sounds like, what code review culture actually enforces, who writes and who edits. Include one artefact per team if you can: a redacted design doc, a real job ad for a role you've filled, a post-mortem you're allowed to share.

There's a visitor segment most careers pages ignore entirely: the client. Prospective clients read careers pages to assess whether the team pitching them is real, stable, and senior. A careers page that shows the work answers their question while answering the candidate's. The [studio page](/studio) and careers page are one argument told twice; make them agree.

## Measurement: the employer brand scoreboard

Employer brand work gets defunded in budget meetings because it's measured in feelings. Measure it properly instead — the careers page has a cleaner funnel than almost any page on your site:

- **Qualified applications per month**, tracked per source, with "qualified" defined harshly in advance. Raw application volume is a vanity metric that rewards bad job ads.
- **Careers-page-to-application rate.** The page's own conversion, the number a redesign should move. Baseline it before changing anything.
- **Offer-acceptance rate and time-to-accept.** The strongest lagging indicator of employer brand: candidates who accept quickly, without a competing-offer saga, were pre-persuaded by the brand.
- **Source-of-hire honesty.** Ask every hire, in writing, what they read before applying. The answers redistribute your content budget better than any audit.
- **The regret metric.** Track six-month regretted attrition, and ask leavers what the gap was between the careers page and the job. That gap — not the page's polish — is your real employer brand debt.

Our own [careers section](/careers) runs this loop, and the discipline is the same as conversion work anywhere else: instrument, hypothesise, change one thing, wait. The metrics in [measuring brand without voodoo](/journal/brand/measuring-brand-health) apply inward too — employer brand is just brand health measured on an audience that joins you.

## Key takeaways

- The careers page is read by candidates, clients, journalists and investors with high intent — treat it as a brand page with a design budget, not an HR noticeboard.
- Enforce voice parity: careers copy in your actual brand voice, read-aloud-tested against your homepage.
- Choose a photography policy — real and annually refreshed, illustrated, or none — and never ship the mixed page.
- Publish salary bands inside a levelling framework, or say plainly why not; silence reads as its own answer.
- Measure qualified applications, page-to-apply conversion and offer-acceptance speed — and close the loop by asking every hire what they read.

## Frequently asked questions

**We're too small for a fancy careers page. Where do we start?**
Start with writing, not design: three honest paragraphs about how the team actually works, real role descriptions with real salary bands, and one artefact of the work. A true, plain page beats an ornate, generic one at any size.

**Won't publishing salary bands cause internal friction?**
It surfaces friction that already exists, which is uncomfortable and useful. The pattern that works: fix the bands internally first, publish second. Publishing broken bands is genuinely worse than silence — but the fix belongs in comp, not in copy.

**How often should the careers page be updated?**
Evergreen structure reviewed quarterly, roles updated continuously, imagery refreshed annually. The signal that matters is freshness: a careers page timestamped 2023 tells candidates more than any of its sentences do.

**Do candidates really read case studies?**
The good ones do, especially senior hires evaluating whether your craft bar matches theirs. Link work liberally; an hour reading your shipped projects is the cheapest mutual due-diligence either side gets.

**What about Glassdoor and external review sites?**
You can't control them; you can only make your own page more specific than the aggregate of strangers. Companies with honest, detailed careers pages find external reviews read in context; companies with stock-photo pages find reviews read as exposé.
