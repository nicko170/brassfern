I've read brand strategies that ran eighty pages. They were impressive in the way a cathedral is impressive — you admire them, then never enter again. Every slideware strategy I've seen fail died the same death: it couldn't be repeated. If the marketing manager can't recite the strategy unprompted in a lift, there is no strategy; there is a document. And documents don't make decisions on a Tuesday afternoon when someone has to choose between two headline options.

So we ship strategy on one page. Not because we're minimalists — because a page is a forcing function. Cramped quarters expose fuzzy thinking the way a word count exposes a wandering essay. Here's the format, the example, and how we get a room of stakeholders to agree to it without bloodshed.

## The page: five blocks, one column of courage

The one-pager has six sections. Five say what the brand is. The sixth — the one everyone wants to skip — says what it will not do.

**1. Audience tension.** Not "who is the audience" — demographics make everyone sleepy. The tension is what they want but can't currently have: *"Finance leads at hospitality groups want clean books without becoming the department that says no to everyone."* A tension is a live wire. A persona is a filing cabinet. If your audience statement contains no conflict, you have a segment, not a strategy.

**2. Position.** One sentence, this exact shape: *For [audience in tension], [brand] is the [category] that [promised outcome], because [reason to believe].* Yes, it's a mad-lib. That's a feature. The sentence is ugly on purpose so nobody falls in love with the phrasing and forgets to argue about the content. Poetry comes later, in the [naming](/journal/brand/naming-process-field-guide) and the [voice charts](/journal/brand/brand-voice-charts). Strategy is allowed to be plain.

**3. Proof.** Three things, no more, that make the promise believable. Proof can be a product truth ("the only ledger that reconciles nightly"), a founder truth ("built by two former CFOs"), or an accumulated truth ("4,000 venues, twelve years"). Three proofs is a stance; seven is a hoard.

**4. Personality.** Three voice positions, not adjectives — "plain-spoken over clever, warm over formal, generous with specifics" — each with one line of consequence. If you've read our piece on [making tone teachable](/journal/brand/brand-voice-charts), this is where those sliders come from. Personality without consequence is décor.

**5. Enemy.** What the brand is against. Not a competitor — a condition. For a community bank: *banking that makes people feel stupid.* For a coffee subscription: *the supermarket-bean compromise.* The enemy is the most rhetorically useful thing on the page; it's what turns position into energy, and it's the single best input to creative work we can hand a design team.

**6. We will not.** Three commitments of refusal. *We will not use jargon a small-business owner would have to google. We will not run discount-led campaigns. We will not ship a feature we can't explain in one sentence.* This column is what makes the rest believable. A promise with no corresponding refusal is just aspiration; the "will not" column is where the strategy grows teeth — and where, later, [brand health audits](/journal/brand/measuring-brand-health) get their "never list" to check the market against.

## An annotated example: Sundial

For context, here's a compressed version of the page behind our [Sundial slow-travel work](/work/sundial-travel-booking) (fictional, like everything on this site — but the arguments were real in every engagement they echo):

- **Tension:** *Well-travelled couples in their 50s–60s want trips that feel discovered, not purchased — but planning slow travel properly takes weeks they don't have.*
- **Position:** *For experienced travellers who've outgrown the package tour, Sundial is the travel planner that designs one journey a season deeply rather than fifty trips shallowly, because every itinerary is walked by a human on our team before it's sold.*
- **Proofs:** (1) Every route walked by staff. (2) A hard cap of twelve departures a year. (3) Founders who ran wilderness lodges for a decade.
- **Personality:** *Unhurried over urgent. Specific over sweeping ("the Thursday market in Apt", never "charming local markets"). A host, not a concierge.*
- **Enemy:** *Itinerary cobbling — the listicle approach to other people's homelands.*
- **We will not:** *sell a trip we haven't walked; use the word "experience" as a noun; run flash sales.*

Read the "we will not" column again. Cap of twelve departures. No flash sales. Those are *commercial* decisions wearing brand clothes, which is exactly right: a strategy that doesn't constrain the business model is a mood board with footnotes.

## Getting the room to agree: facilitation notes

The page is the easy part. The room is the work. After running these workshops for a decade, the moves that matter:

**Sequence the argument: tension before position.** Stakeholders who disagree about the position often actually disagree about the audience tension. Surface the tension first and get explicit sign-off — a literal show of hands — before moving on. It converts later position-fights into reference checks: "does this serve the tension we agreed?"

**Run the "will not" column as an auction.** Ask each stakeholder to write three refusals privately, then pitch them. The room votes; the top three survive. Private writing first is non-negotiable — otherwise the loudest voice drafts the brand's spine. Expect the CEO's list to be the longest and the hardest to cut. Cutting it in front of the room is the moment the strategy becomes real, because everyone watches what leadership is willing to give up.

**Test the position against a real decision.** Take a live question from the backlog — a partnership offer, a sponsorship, a pricing idea — and run it through the draft page in the workshop. If the page doesn't resolve the question in ninety seconds, it isn't strategy yet. We bring three live decisions to every workshop for exactly this purpose. Strategy that can't arbitrate is poetry.

**One owner, one meeting.** The page needs a single accountable owner (usually the founder or CMO) and a standing fifteen-minute slot in some existing meeting where decisions get checked against it. Strategy without a recurring venue is a poster.

**Don't wordsmith in the room.** The room argues substance; two people polish phrasing afterward. Wordsmithing by committee is how you get "empowering journeys of discovery" — a sentence that has never caused a single sale.

## Where the one-pager sits in the system

The page is the trunk; everything else is branches. Voice charts expand block 4. [Naming and identity](/services/brand-identity) expand the position into form. The website's homepage is the position rendered in type and space. Paid creative is the enemy, dramatised. When the branches drift — and they drift, constantly — the audit question is always the same: *which line of the page does this artefact serve?* Artefacts with no answer get pruned.

This is also why the one-pager belongs inside every [discovery sprint](/journal/playbooks/discovery-sprint-playbook): two weeks is enough time to draft it, pressure-test it against real decisions, and get the room to sign it — and it gives every downstream deliverable a fixed point to orient against.

## Failure modes we see quarterly

- **The target is "everyone."** Tension requires a somebody. "Ambitious teams everywhere" is not an audience; it's a horoscope.
- **Proofs that are promises.** "We're committed to quality" is not proof. Proof withstands the question "show me."
- **The enemy is a competitor.** Naming Acme Corp as the enemy makes your strategy hostage to their roadmap. Fight a condition, not a company.
- **The "will not" column is all trivial.** "We will not use clipart." Braver please. The refusals should cost something, or they're etiquette, not strategy.
- **Nobody owns the page.** If the one-pager lives in a deck from an offsite, it died at the offsite.

## Key takeaways

- Strategy must be repeatable to be useful. One page is the unit of repeatability.
- Sequence: audience tension → position → proof → personality → enemy → refusals. Each block arbitrates the next one down.
- The "we will not" column is the strategy's teeth — refusals with real commercial cost.
- Test the draft page against three live decisions in the workshop; if it can't arbitrate, it isn't done.
- Give the page an owner and a recurring meeting, or frame it and admit it's art.

## FAQ

**Can this work for a company with multiple product lines?**
Yes — one page for the company, then one-page *stanzas* per product line that inherit the enemy and personality but carry their own tension and position. If a product line needs a different enemy, it's not a product line; it's a brand, and you're making an [architecture decision](/journal/brand/brand-architecture-decisions).

**How long does it take to get to a signed one-pager?**
Typically two to four weeks: customer interviews and internal listening in week one, a half-day workshop in week two, a decision-testing round and polish after. The bottleneck is never the writing — it's scheduling the people whose refusals matter.

**Should customers ever see the one-pager?**
The page itself is an internal instrument, but its contents should be embarrassingly visible in the work: the enemy in the copy, the proofs on the homepage, the "will nots" felt in the pricing page and the product roadmap. If customers can't reconstruct your strategy from your site, the page is theoretical.

**How often should the page change?**
Review annually, change reluctantly. Audiences and tensions evolve; enemies and personalities should be load-bearing. A page revised quarterly was never signed properly — it was a draft with a meeting cadence.

**What's the difference between this and a brand platform / pyramid / onion?**
Mostly compression and consequence. The classic frameworks carry the same atoms but bury them across forty slides, and almost none include the refusals column. We kept the atoms, added the teeth, and made it fit where decisions actually happen: one page, one glance.
