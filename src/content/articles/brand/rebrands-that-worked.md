A rebrand is the most expensive unsupervised bet most companies ever make. A logo, a palette, a launch video, a surge of LinkedIn applause — and then, six weeks later, the same conversion rate, the same churn, the same awkward sales calls, in nicer colours. We've been on both sides of that outcome enough times to say it plainly: rebrands work when they're the *visible edge of a deeper change*, and they fail, expensively, when they're paint on a business that needed plumbing.

This is our working anatomy of the difference, drawn from eleven years of doing it (and twice talking clients out of it).

## First: is it a rebrand, a refresh, or a cry for help?

Before any work, we force a diagnosis. Three categories, three different jobs:

**A refresh** keeps the name, the structure and the recognition, and modernises the execution — tightened wordmark, systematic colour, typography that can actually render on the web. Refresh when recognition is an asset and the system has sprawl damage: seventeen blues, four logo variants, a PDF from 2019. Most companies asking for a rebrand need this.

**A rebrand** changes the positioning the identity expresses — new audience, new category, new promise, sometimes a new name. You rebrand when the *story customers would tell about you* has diverged from the one your materials tell. The identity becomes the announcement of a change that has already happened in the business.

**A cry for help** is when "we need a rebrand" actually means "sales feel hard" or "a competitor looks sharper." New paint never fixes this. Our rule, stated early and kindly: if we swapped your logo for your competitor's and nothing about the customer experience would change, identity isn't your bottleneck. Spend the money on the product, the pricing page or the [funnel](/journal/growth/funnel-metrics-that-matter), and rebrand later with evidence.

## The ones that worked — three anatomies

Our strongest rebrands shared a spine: the strategy was settled *before* design started, and the rollout was treated as its own project with its own budget.

**The heritage modernisation.** When [Tallow & Co.](/work/tallow-and-co-providore) came to us, they were a 1987 butcher with queues out the door and a website from eleven years earlier. The wrong move would have been to sand off the history and chase the artisan-deli aesthetic. The right move was to make the heritage *legible to a younger customer*: keep the name's plainness, build typography that felt printed rather than designed, and photograph the actual shop rather than stock food. Illustrative outcome: same-day-delivery orders grew 68% in the first season, and — the number we cared about more — the average online customer's age dropped eleven years without the in-store base flinching. The rebrand worked because it translated rather than replaced.

**The category escape.** A fictional-but-typical pattern from our B2B work: a payroll tool that had quietly become a people-operations platform, still wearing the name and washed-teal identity of its compliance origins. Their sales calls started with twenty minutes of "we're actually more than payroll." The rebrand's job was to kill that preamble. Name kept (recognition in finance is gold), positioning rewritten around one sentence, identity rebuilt to feel like infrastructure for humans rather than software for rules. The metric that proved it wasn't the launch-week traffic spike; it was sales-call time-to-demo dropping by a third, quarter over quarter. That's a rebrand earning its invoice.

**The merger untangling.** Two acquired products, three sub-brands, a naming system nobody could explain in a lift. This one was mostly [architecture, not aesthetics](/journal/brand/brand-architecture-decisions) — the hard graft of deciding what endorses what, what gets retired, and which legacy names had equity worth the awkwardness. The visual system came last and almost casualty-free, because every stakeholder had already fought the real battles on the naming map. Takeaway: when an identity project is genuinely an architecture project, do it as one, and let the logo be the epilogue.

Notice what none of these led with: a logo. The mark was the *last* artefact to fall out of each project, which is roughly our position in [the logo is dead; long live the identity system](/journal/brand/logo-is-dead-system).

## The paint jobs — honest post-mortems

We've also shipped work that didn't move the needle, and the taxonomy of failure is consistent:

**Strategy hand-waved, design over-delivered.** Beautiful system, gorgeous launch film, and a positioning line — "empowering modern teams to do their best work" — that could sit on any of a thousand sites. When the strategy page could be true of everyone, it's true of no one, and no kerning will save it. We now refuse to open Figma until the [one-page strategy](/journal/brand/brand-strategy-one-pager) survives a hostile reading by the client's own sales lead.

**Rollout as afterthought.** The identity launched on the website and everywhere else lagged by a year: old deck templates, old email footers, old signage photographed against the new site in the same press coverage. Customers don't experience a brand in the keynote; they experience it in the tenth touchpoint. This is why we budget rollout as a first-class phase — the unglamorous, crucial middle we've written about in our [rebrand rollout plan](/journal/brand/rebrand-rollout-plan). A rebrand that ships to 40% of touchpoints is 40% believed.

**The founder's rebrand.** Identity aimed at impressing the board rather than orienting the customer. Tell-tale: the brief uses "we" twenty times and "they" twice. The customer should be able to feel what changed for *them* — clearer promise, easier buying, better support — or the whole exercise is interior decoration.

## Sequencing: the order that keeps rebrands cheap

The expensive rebrands are the ones done in the wrong order. Ours, after a decade of corrections:

1. **Strategy in one page, argued to death.** Audience, promise, proof, personality. If it takes forty slides, it's undecided.
2. **Verbal before visual.** Name and voice first — they're the surfaces customers touch most and change slowest. Voice, especially, is cheaper to get right early than to retrofit; see how we [hand voice over](/journal/brand/brand-voice-survives-handover) so it survives us.
3. **Identity as system.** Wordmark last of the visible things; colour, type, motion and layout logic first, because they carry 95% of the exposure.
4. **Rollout plan with owners and dates**, sequenced by customer exposure — the billing email matters more than the office wall.
5. **Measurement baseline taken before launch**, so "did it work?" is an answerable question rather than a vibe.

## Did it work? Measuring without voodoo

Launch-week metrics are confetti. Real measures, tracked for two quarters: branded search volume (are more people looking for you by name?), direct traffic trend, sales-cycle friction (time-to-demo, preamble length), support-ticket sentiment on comms-heavy moments, and price realisation — can you hold or raise prices without conversion falling? If none of those moved, you bought paint. We keep the full honest-instrument list in [measuring brand without voodoo](/journal/brand/measuring-brand-health).

The uncomfortable truth is that a rebrand's value mostly shows up as *reduced drag*: clearer first impressions, shorter explanations, fewer "so what do you actually do?" moments. Drag is measurable, if you're willing to look.

## Key takeaways

- Diagnose before you design: refresh, rebrand or cry for help are three different jobs with three different budgets.
- Rebrand only when the positioning changed — the identity announces a change that already happened in the business.
- Verbal identity before visual; the mark is the last artefact, not the first.
- Budget the rollout as its own phase with owners and dates; a rebrand shipped to 40% of touchpoints is 40% believed.
- Baseline measurement before launch, judge after two quarters: branded search, sales friction, support sentiment, price realisation.
- If the strategy fits on a slide that any competitor could also claim, stop. That's the project's real cost.

## FAQ

### How much does a rebrand cost versus a refresh?

Roughly, a refresh costs 30–50% of a full rebrand because the strategy is settled and recognition is preserved. A true rebrand — positioning, naming work, verbal identity, system, rollout — is the single biggest brand spend most SMEs will make, which is exactly why we're so stubborn about the diagnosis. Paying rebrand prices for a paint job is the industry's most common waste.

### Should we rebrand before or after a funding round?

After the strategy is settled, whenever that falls. Investors fund clarity, not novelty; a rebrand timed purely to a raise often papers over positioning questions the diligence process will re-open anyway. If the rebrand genuinely sharpens the story you'll tell investors, do it three to six months early so launch noise settles before rooms.

### How long should a rebrand take from kickoff to launch?

Four to nine months for a mid-size company, and the variance is almost entirely decision latency, not design time. Two-week stakeholder feedback rounds double the calendar. The single best schedule protector is a weekly decision meeting with the one person who can actually say yes in the room.

### What if we hate the new identity six months in?

First check whether you hate the *identity* or the *change itself* — internal fatigue with a new system peaks around month four and is usually about adoption pain, not quality. If the metrics are moving and the system is holding together at the touchpoints, hold your nerve. Re-rebranding inside two years reads as instability to everyone, and the second attempt rarely fixes what the first one fumbled.
