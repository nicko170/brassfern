Nothing stress-tests a brand like sharing. The identity that behaved beautifully on your own website, under the control of your own team, suddenly sits next to another company's identity — with its own typefaces, its own colour ego, its own legal team, and an agency of its own that thinks *their* logo should lead. Co-branding is where brand systems either prove they were designed as systems or reveal they were only ever a logo and a hope.

We've designed partnership kits for conferences, joint products, co-marketing campaigns and one memorably tense three-way sponsorship. The pattern repeats: the visual questions are solvable in a week, and the *political* questions detonate in week six — unless someone drafts the rules early. This is that draft.

## First question: what kind of co-brand is this?

Before geometry, taxonomy. "Co-branding" covers at least four structurally different situations, and they need different rules:

**Endorsement.** One brand presents, the other validates — "Product X, powered by Platform Y." Hierarchy is settled by the relationship itself. The endorser's mark is smaller, subordinate, and usually non-negotiable in placement because their legal team has thought about this more than yours has.

**Collaboration.** Two brands create a campaign or event together, as peers. This is where lockup geometry matters most, because hierarchy must be *negotiated*, not derived.

**Venue.** Your brand appears inside someone else's environment — a conference, a marketplace, an app directory. You don't control the frame; you control what you hand over. The deliverable is a well-prepared asset pack, not a lockup.

**Marriage.** A joint venture or merged product gets its own identity drawing from both parents. That's not co-branding at all; it's a naming and identity project where the parents are stakeholders. Treat it as one, with the full process — we wrote about the naming half in [our naming field guide](/journal/brand/naming-process-field-guide).

Half the fights we've untangled came from two partners assuming different frames from this list. Establish the taxonomy in the kickoff email, not the sixth revision.

## Lockup geometry: the physics of two logos

A co-brand lockup is two marks, a separator, and spacing — and every one of the four elements carries politics.

**The order question.** Who leads? For collaborations, the honest answer is often *contextual*: whoever owns the channel leads on that channel. Your partner's logo leads on their landing page; yours leads on yours; the joint microsite flipbooks it or uses the event identity instead. Writing this down converts an ego standoff into a table of ten rows that both teams can approve in one meeting.

**The separator.** A thin vertical rule is the default because it's neutral — a hinge, not a bullet point. Avoid the temptation to design a bespoke "connector glyph"; it becomes a third brand asset nobody owns and everybody resents. For endorsements, a word often beats a glyph entirely: "by", "powered by", "in partnership with" set in the *presenter's* typeface, which quietly reinforces the hierarchy.

**Optical sizing, not equal sizing.** Two logos set at identical heights rarely look equal — a compact wordmark shouts next to a tall, airy symbol. Size marks to *optical weight*, adjusting until neither dominates at arm's length, then bake the ratio into the lockup so nobody re-eyeballs it per deliverable. We set the ratio as relative units in the asset pack itself; when the files travel, the judgement travels with them.

**Clear space is shared custody.** Define a clear-space unit derived from one mark (usually the larger), apply it around the *whole lockup* as a single object, and state explicitly that partners may not separate, reorder or recolour the components. The failure mode we're legislating against is the well-meaning marketing manager who ungroups the SVG to "make it fit the banner."

## Colour custody: whose brand owns the canvas?

Logos are the easy part. Colour is where co-brands actually fight, because colour is territory. Three governance patterns, in increasing order of sophistication:

**Neutral ground.** Joint assets live on a deliberately neutral canvas — white, black, or a shared neutral neither brand claims — with each logo in its own full colour. Boring, bulletproof, correct for most collaborations. If both brands are chromatically loud, neutral ground isn't a compromise; it's the only readable option.

**The borrowed bend.** The channel owner's palette dominates, and the partner brand appears in monochrome or a single approved tint that harmonises without capitulating. This needs a pre-agreed "reduced palette" per brand: which single colour survives the journey into foreign territory? Decide this once, centrally, and every channel fight afterwards gets shorter.

**The third space.** Long-running partnerships develop a hybrid accent — a colour that belongs to the *partnership itself*, distinct from either parent. This works when the collaboration has its own name and recurring presence (an annual summit, an ongoing programme). It's the strongest signal that the relationship is real, and the most expensive to maintain, which is exactly why it persuades.

One rule overrides all three: never put one brand's logo on a field of the other's signature colour unless the relationship is explicitly endorsement-tier. To the audience's eye, colour field reads as ownership, and misreading ownership has genuine legal-adjacent consequences — which brings us to the part designers like to pretend is someone else's job.

## The legal-adjacent bits designers should draft early

We are designers, not lawyers, and the trademark licence is counsel's document. But in every smooth co-brand we've shipped, the design team drafted a *one-page usage annex* alongside the creative — translated legalese into the practical questions, and surfaced the gaps while everyone was still friendly. The annex covers:

- **Approval workflow.** Who signs off co-branded assets on each side, with response-time expectations ("3 business days, silence is not consent"). Undefined approval is how campaigns miss launch dates while both sides wait for the other.
- **Version custody.** Where the canonical lockup files live, who may distribute them, and which version is current. Link to the living guidelines, not to a zip in an email — the same argument we make for [killing the brand PDF](/journal/brand/brand-guidelines-living) applies double in partnerships.
- **Sunset terms.** What happens to assets when the partnership ends: takedown timelines, whether archived content may stay live, and whether the marks may appear in portfolio and case-study contexts afterwards. Nobody asks this at the honeymoon stage; everybody wishes they had at the divorce.
- **Modification rights.** Explicitly enumerate the permitted adaptations — monochrome variants, minimum sizes, dark-mode swaps — and prohibit everything else by default. Enumerated permissions read as helpful; vague silence reads as permission.

Drafting this page early is a service to the lawyers, not a trespass: counsel edits a concrete page in twenty minutes, versus inventing one under deadline with a launch hanging on it.

## Templates beat rules

Even perfect rules fail if applying them takes effort, so the deliverable that actually governs a partnership is a **template kit**: the lockup pre-composed at common aspect ratios (16:9 slide, 1:1 social, 9:16 story, email header, event banner), in light and dark, as locked frames in Figma and as PNG/SVG exports with sensible filenames.

Every template is a decision nobody has to make again. The kit we built for Holloway Records' festival partnerships — the same thinking that shaped [their label site](/work/holloway-records-label-site) — shipped twelve locked frames and a one-page annex. A year later, not one rogue lockup had surfaced, which in partnership terms is a spotless audit.

Budget the follow-through, too: a six-month asset review where both teams walk recent live materials together. Partnerships drift not through malice but through turnover — the person who attended the brand workshop leaves, and their successor inherits the zip without the judgement. A recurring review is how the rules survive the people.

## Key takeaways

- Classify the partnership first — endorsement, collaboration, venue, or marriage — because each needs structurally different co-brand rules.
- Solve logo order with a channel table, not a hierarchy war; bake optical sizing ratios into the lockup itself.
- Govern colour with neutral ground, borrowed-bend reduced palettes, or a partnership-owned third space — decided before the first asset, not during it.
- Draft the one-page usage annex early: approvals, custody, modification rights, and sunset terms are design problems wearing legal clothes.
- Ship locked templates for the twelve placements that matter; templates govern where rules only advise.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which logo goes first in a co-brand lockup?**
There's no universal answer, and the fight is the problem. Agree that channel owners lead on their own channels, split joint channels deliberately, and document the table. "First" is a reading-order question, and readers mostly notice *imbalance*, not order.

**How do we handle a partner whose brand clashes badly with ours?**
Neutral ground plus optical sizing solves ninety percent of clashes. For the rest, agree reduced palettes — each brand nominates one colour that travels — and keep signatures off each other's signature coloured fields.

**Can we change our logo's colour to match a partner's?**
Almost never, and the annex should say so. A recoloured logo is a new trademark use with legal weight, and it trains audiences to misread ownership. Monochrome knockouts on neutral fields are the sanctioned exception.

**Who owns co-branded campaign assets afterwards?**
Specify in the agreement: typically each party may retain and display the work with attribution, but neither may *reuse the partner's marks* in new materials after the term ends without fresh approval. Get it in writing before the campaign, not after the award entry.

**How much should co-branding guidelines cost to produce?**
A lightweight collaboration kit — lockups, colour rules, one-page annex, core templates — is days of work, not weeks. If the partnership is worth announcing, it's worth the small cost of not improvising it in public.
