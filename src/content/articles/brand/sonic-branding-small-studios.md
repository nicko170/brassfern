Somewhere right now, a product team is in a brand workshop being shown a slide with a sound wave on it. The agency will describe the brand's "audio essence," someone will play a 30-second ident through laptop speakers, and the team will nod politely and never think about it again. Six-figure sonic identity programs have a spectacular irrelevance rate, because most products are used on mute, most podcasts are skipped at the intro, and a brand sound with no considered surface is a logo for an empty room.

And yet: sound is the most under-brewed layer of nearly every brand. Done small, it's cheap, fast to ship, and — precisely because nobody bothers — disproportionately distinctive. This is the version that works at startup scale: three moments, one motif, honest defaults.

## The three-moment rule

A small sonic identity covers exactly three moments, chosen for *audibility* — the moments where it's plausible someone actually hears you:

1. **The confirmation.** A payment completes, a file uploads, a door unlocks in your smart-home app. A 200–400ms resolved sound on the moments the user most wants closure. This is the highest-frequency, highest-emotion surface you own.
2. **The arrival.** The app opens (only if someone would ever open it with sound on), a video intro sting, the first beat of your event talk, a [podcast intro](/work/signal-and-noise-podcast-network) that regulars can hum.
3. **The signature.** One place where the sound is pure brand rather than interface: a two-second audio logo on video end-cards, the hold music, the chime in the TV spot. One place. Signature sounds lose value per repetition — rarity is the point, same law as [logo systems](/journal/brand/logo-systems-not-logos) learn too late.

Everything else — scroll sounds, hover sounds, typing sounds, the infamous chat ping that sounds like a notification from a *different* app — is banned by default until someone argues it in. Sound has the strongest attention claim of any channel; spending it on trivia is how you train users to mute you forever, which in sound branding is called death.

## UI sound principles that don't get you muted

Interface sound is governed by the same physics as [motion design](/journal/web-design/motion-that-earns-its-keep): it earns its keep or gets cut. The rules we write into brand sound systems:

- **Information, not decoration.** A sound must tell you something your eyes might have missed: success vs failure (these must never be confusable), arrival vs departure, your app vs the OS. A sound that means nothing means "turn all sounds off."
- **Shorter than feels necessary.** 300ms reads as confident; 900ms reads as a screensaver. Professionals design in tens of milliseconds and it shows.
- **Quiet by construction.** UI sounds mix at -20dB-ish relative to content; the mix is part of the design file, not a user problem. If you haven't heard your sounds on laptop speakers at 40% volume, you haven't heard your sounds.
- **Harmonic family.** Every sound in the set is built from the same scale, same instrument family, same envelope character — the audio equivalent of a [type system](/journal/brand/typography-brand-distinctiveness). Three sounds that relate to each other sound designed. Three unrelated sounds sound like a settings menu.
- **Neutral states, emotional resolution.** Ambient and neutral cues sit mid-register and unobtrusive; only the confirmation and the error get a strong gesture — one resolving upward, one clearly not. Users build the map in a week if the map is consistent.

## Accessibility and respect: the defaults that decide everything

Sonic branding lives or dies on consent and compatibility; get these wrong and the other 95% doesn't matter.

**Off by default unless moments of explicit expectation.** Media-rich contexts — video, calls, the [music player](/lab) class of products — can ship sound on. Everything else: sounds arrive off or at system default, with a persistent, findable toggle that isn't buried three settings deep. Respect for ambient silence is the brand.

**Never the only channel.** Any information the sound carries must also arrive visually or haptically. Testing the sound set with screen readers nearby, in hearing-loss contexts, in open offices, on a train — sound that duplicates rather than replaces is how it's meant to work.

**Respect the system.** OS-level sound settings, Do Not Disturb, silent switches — the app honours all of them, every time, no growth-team "engagement" exception. And `prefers-reduced-motion` users have made a statement about sensory load; honour the spirit, not just the rule.

## Commissioning one motif, properly

The budget move that takes sonic identity from wallpaper to brand: commission *one* motif — two to four pitches of 0.5–1.5 seconds — and derive the entire system from it. The confirmation is the motif resolving. The arrival is the motif establishing. The signature sting is the motif, full stop.

- **Write the audio brief like a design brief**: three reference tracks with markups ("the attack here, not this reverb tail"), the emotions named as *situations* not genres ("the moment an invoice pays" not "uplifting corporate"), and the forbiddens — no risers, no whooshes, no THX-sized gestures. Forbiddens are the moat, exactly as in [illustration systems](/journal/brand/illustration-systems-not-galleries).
- **Hire a composer, not a library.** Stock audio is someone else's system rented by the month, and your "brand sound" will appear in a mattress ad by autumn. A freelance composer building a three-sound microsystem is a four-figure job; it's the best money in brand.
- **Deliverables**: stems, alternate lengths (150/300/600ms for UI, 2s and 5s for signature), mixed at broadcast-loudness targets, in a living folder next to the rest of your [brand guidelines](/journal/brand/brand-guidelines-living) with a one-page usage sheet — where each sound may play, at what gain, with which visual.

## Where sonic branding is just vanity

Honesty round: some products shouldn't spend a dollar here. If your product is used on mute 99% of the time (a B2B dashboard, most e-commerce admin), in silence-demanding contexts (health settings, libraries of anxiety), or if your brand has zero audio-adjacent surfaces — no video, no events, no app, no podcast — then sonic identity is a workshop deliverable, not a brand layer. Spend the money on the moments people actually experience. The clearest test: name the moment a customer would *hear* your brand this week. If you can't, mute the project. Done right the other way around, though — one motif, three moments, defaults of respect — sound is the cheapest distinctiveness on the market, because almost nobody else in your category bothered to tune their small brass.

## Key takeaways

- Cover three moments only: the confirmation, the arrival, the signature. Everything else is banned by default.
- UI sounds carry information, run under 400ms, mix quiet, and share one harmonic family — or they train users to mute you.
- Sound is off by default outside media moments, never the sole channel for information, and always respects OS silence settings.
- Commission one motif from a composer and derive the whole system; stock audio is rented distinctiveness.
- The vanity test: name the moment a customer hears your brand this week. No moment, no spend.

## FAQ

**Won't users just turn sounds off anyway?**
Some will, and the design lets them in one tap — that's the system working. The finding that matters: users mute *meaningless* sound categorically and keep *useful* sound, especially confirmations, on quiet. Earn the 300ms and you keep the channel open; waste it once on a hover chirp and the channel closes forever.

**Should our sonic identity match our playlist/Spotify-brand "vibe"?**
Playlists curation is mood marketing, fine as content, but it's not a sonic identity — identity is the composed, ownable, repeatable motif, not a genre you rent by association. Don't let a workshop sell you a playlist as strategy; the test is whether you could hum *yours*.

**How do we test whether the sounds work?**
Small and honest: five-second exposure tests (play the confirmation, ask "what happened?"), confusability checks between your success and error cues on laptop speakers at low volume, and a two-week live beta watching the sound-toggle rate. If 30% disable within a fortnight, the sounds are wrong; iterate the mix, not the toggle's discoverability.

**We already have an audio logo from a previous brand push. Can it be salvaged?**
Usually. Extract its strongest 1–2 pitches as the motif — the interval is the identity, not the orchestration — rederive the UI set from it at proper lengths and mixes, and retire the 12-second cinematic version to video end-cards only. Most "audio logos" are stingers; yours wants to be an interval that survives at 200 milliseconds.
