Ledgerline is a mid-size accounting and advisory practice in Chippendale, Sydney — forty-two accountants, a client list full of hospitality groups and light manufacturers, and a reputation among its clients that can only be described as devoted. Business owners recommend them at barbecues. Their partners give the kind of blunt, generous advice that gets quoted back years later. And their website, in 2024, said approximately none of this. It said "solutions" and "tailored" and "a full range of services", in a stock-photo palette of what one partner accurately called "conference-room blue".

The firm came to us with a brief that was really a confession. Referrals had carried them for nineteen years, but the incoming generation of founders googled first and asked around second. Ledgerline was losing pitch shortlists to firms with worse advice and better words. Their managing partner, Rosa Deane, put it plainly in the kickoff: "We're interesting in the room and invisible outside it. Fix the outside."

Metrics in this piece are illustrative — Ledgerline is fictional, like everything on this site — but the shape of the problem is one we meet constantly in professional services.

## The challenge

Three weeks of discovery — partner interviews, client calls, a full content audit — surfaced four failures, in ascending order of how much they cost:

- **The voice had been sanded off.** Ledgerline's advice culture was strong opinions, plainly held. Their written output was the opposite: hedged, abstract, indistinguishable from every mid-tier firm. The gap between how they talked and how they wrote was the brand problem in miniature.
- **Ninety-one "insights" that answered nothing.** The news section was company announcements and tax-date reminders. The questions clients actually rang about — hire-through-a-trust, R&D claims for small manufacturers, what to do when your bookkeeper quits mid-quarter — existed only in email threads and hallway conversations.
- **The identity had no opinion of its own.** A swoosh-adjacent mark from 2011, three blues fighting in one palette, and a typeface that appeared to have apologised for something. Nothing to build on, nothing worth defending.
- **Zero attribution.** Nobody knew what the website did for revenue, because nothing on the website was designed to be measured. Enquiries arrived as "source: unknown" and everyone had stopped asking.

The strategic question underneath: does a refresh mean new paint, or new behaviour? We argued hard for the latter. Our thinking on the difference is written up in [refresh or rebrand?](/journal/brand/brand-refresh-vs-rebrand) — and Ledgerline became the case study we now point to for "new behaviour".

## The approach

**Brand strategy that fits on one page.** We ran our standard compression exercise with the partners: sixty claim cards on a wall, argue until nine survive. What emerged was a one-page strategy — our house rule, described in [brand strategy on one page](/journal/brand/brand-strategy-one-pager) — centred on a line the partners had been saying to clients for years without noticing it was positioning: *"We tell you what we'd do."* Advice as the product. Conviction as the differentiator. Everything else — naming, identity, site, content program — hung off that spike.

**A voice chart before a wordmark.** Most identity projects start with the logo. We started with a voice chart: four axes (plain↔ornate, warm↔cool, certain↔hedged, dry↔witty), where Ledgerline sits, and — crucially — real before-and-after rewrites of their own emails and advice letters. The partners could see what "certain but warm" looked like in a sentence about payroll tax within the first workshop. Buy-in followed because the artefact was useful on day one, not a mood board of somebody else's brands.

**Identity: ledgers, taken seriously.** The visual system grew from the firm's own material history — ruled ledger paper, tabular figures, the rubber stamp. A wordmark in a sharp transitional serif, a palette of ledger green and brass on warm paper, hairline rules everywhere, and a system of "margin notes" — short asides set in the gutter, borrowed from how partners actually annotate documents. It looks like accounting the way accounting feels when it's done well: orderly, opinionated, faintly dry. No swooshes were harmed because none were invited.

**The publishing engine.** This was the real work, and the part most refreshes skip. We rebuilt the site on React and TypeScript with Sanity underneath, but the CMS was the easy half. The hard half was the operating model: we interviewed twelve partners and senior managers and mined ~200 anonymised client questions from their own email archives, then built an editorial pipeline around answering them. Each insight piece starts as a real client question, gets drafted by the relevant partner in a 45-minute interview with our content lead, and ships with a named byline, a worked example, and a "what we'd do" box at the end. The byline rule was non-negotiable: no ghost corporate we. Partners write because partners are the product.

**Structure for search, conversion by honesty.** Insights were organised into clusters — starting a business, running payroll, selling up, and so on — matching how owners actually search, per our notes on [content clusters that compound](/journal/growth/content-clusters-strategy). Conversion paths were rebuilt around one honest promise: enquire, and a partner replies within one business day. The enquiry form asks five questions, not eighteen, and states the response promise in plain text next to the button. Qualified enquiries beat raw form fills as the target metric, agreed with Rosa up front — a firm of forty-two cannot absorb, and does not want, tyre-kicker volume.

**Migration without a dip.** Ninety-one legacy URLs were mapped, redirected or retired; the handful of genuinely useful old pieces were rewritten into the new voice rather than abandoned. The rebuild shipped behind our usual pre-launch SEO gates, with rankings on tracked terms held flat through cutover. The engagement ran on our fixed-scope sprints with a light content retainer behind it — the shape described on our [pricing page](/pricing) and, like everything we ship, visible to the client in working builds from week two per [our approach](/approach).

## The outcome

Twelve months of post-launch data, illustrative like everything here but shaped like the truth:

- **Qualified inbound enquiries up 58%** against the prior year, with "organic search" finally appearing in the source column — 34% of new enquiries, from a standing start of effectively zero.
- **Time on insights roughly tripled** (1:12 to 3:35 average engaged time), and the byline experiment worked: individual partners now have followings, and "I read Priya's piece on trust structures" is a sentence said to the intake team about weekly.
- **Two unsolicited pitch invitations** arrived in the first six months explicitly citing the insights section — the shortlist problem, inverted.
- **The content program survived us.** This is the metric we care about most. The publishing cadence — two substantial pieces a month — continued through a full financial year after handover, because the model (real questions, interview-drafted, partner bylines) was built around forty minutes of a partner's time, not their weekend.

"The old website was a brochure we apologised for," Rosa said at the year review. "The new one argues with people before we meet them. Half the first meeting is already done." That's the whole thesis: in professional services, your content is your conversation, started early, at scale, with your own accent intact.
